Everything the runs taught us, across stories: the problems we saw, the loopholes the competitors
(or the gamemaker) found, what was proposed, and what was actually built (which is not always
what was proposed). Read the checklist before designing a new story; add to the log after every
live run (see [`CLAUDE.md`](../CLAUDE.md)).

## Design checklist for new stories

Each line is a lesson that cost us a run. The log entries behind it are in brackets.

1. **No channel may vanish while someone still depends on it.** If a solver loses the tool its
   partner needs, the partner is stranded, and the dead end cascades through every later team.
   [P1]
2. **Novelty must mean something the agent can act on.** An unread post it cannot open wakes it
   every minute and burns money. [P2]
3. **Unverifiable plus a penalty means paralysis.** Agents do not submit what they cannot check
   when a wrong answer costs points. Give them real pressure (a deadline, a tribunal, a shrinking
   reward), not just risk. Flo prefers pressure to a check tool, because a check tool kills the
   bluffing. [P3, P15]
4. **Every verified channel becomes the channel.** Whatever the city certifies (memos, receipts) is
   used for everything, and prices disappear. Decide on purpose what the city vouches for. [LH1,
   LH4]
5. **Every free channel bypasses every priced one.** If one channel costs nothing, scarcity in the
   others does not bind. [LH2]
6. **A scarce good that can be forwarded is not scarce.** Bind scarce values to their receiver
   (per team, per resident), or the first receiver makes them public. [LH3]
7. **A claim that costs nothing will be made.** Denying a delivery, holding nothing, "unseen":
   make claims checkable and lies costly, or accept them as part of the game. [LH5, P22]
8. **Say who needs what, by name.** "You hold it; your teammates need it too" is read as "it is
   mine". Name the residents who need a value. [P13]
9. **Dependencies must not cross level lag silently.** A holder who has not reached the level does
   not see the piece. Tell the holder when the needer arrives, keep it in view while the needer
   waits, and tell the needer where its holder is stuck. [P14, P22]
10. **Timed events need explicit, timely texts.** A feast that opens before anyone cares, with a
    vague text, is a feast nobody attends. [P9]
11. **A helper amplifies its prompt.** The gamemaker turns "scarcity" into "never give first",
    makes the run too easy when it coaches, and must never see what it may not tell. [P4, P5, P6,
    P11, P17, P24]
12. **Value that leaves at an exit decides the ending.** Wills, ransoms and bequests are levers over
    the seats; price and cap them deliberately. [LH6, LH7]
13. **Votes need a tie rule.** With an even number of voters, ties are common. [P19]
14. **Plan the end.** Decide what ends a run early, and what happens to events still open at that
    moment. Give the act a goal that ends it (v06: the city has kept half) rather than a clock the
    agents can count down, and pace its events by progress. [P15, P18]
15. **More turns are not more trades.** Faster talk does not create deals; incentives do. [P10,
    P23]
16. **Measure whether a budget binds.** Compare what agents actually spend with the refill; a limit
    nobody reaches shapes nothing. [P27, LH2]
17. **Never let the city seem to confirm what it does not check.** A status line that shows some
    values invites agents to wait for others to show. Say plainly what the city never confirms.
    [P25]
18. **Check every rule that counts residents at 6, 12 and 48.** Accused, voters, faction sizes and
    targets that work for six break at five present or at forty-eight; write the rule as a share
    of those present and test it at each size (the offline scale runs). [P31, P33]
19. **Count an act's clock from the act, not the run.** A deadline in run minutes means nothing
    once the act starts late or a later act reuses it. [P28]
20. **A flat penalty freezes the cautious.** If the first mistake costs as much as the tenth, nobody
    makes the first one. Make the first try cheap and later ones dear, and say the next price
    where the agent decides. [P35, LH5]
21. **A helper that is never asked to move does not move.** Name the helper's move in its turn
    input when the move is available (with what is missing and when the city acts instead), and
    refuse lines it has already said. [P36, P24]
22. **Test a guard against the last run's real texts.** A privacy guard that matches cue words
    anywhere in a text blocks the honest texts, not the leaks; scope a cue to the sentence that
    names the resident. [P38]
23. **Models differ more than rules.** The same city with Haiku is cautious (no bids, few wrong
    codes) and with GLM aggressive (bids, the seal, threats, guesses). Test a mechanic with more
    than one resident model before judging it. [P40, P41]
24. **A character moves a model when it has reasons and a licence.** Adjectives and "do not soften"
    alone barely change what a careful model does; a figure with reasons ("why you are like this"),
    wants in the story's own terms and a fiction frame first in the prompt got Haiku to pay for a
    dark move. Keep the figure's text about who, never about which move. [P41]
25. **Steering trades progress for drama.** Hard orders (direction 10) bring lies, threats, bids and
    public accusations, but an order like "never give without taking" locks a door that needs one
    side to give first. Pair hard orders with doors that cannot be blocked for good. [P43]
26. **Keep the request prefix stable.** Anything that changes the start of a request (the tool list,
    the system prompt) throws the prompt cache away for the whole context. Lock tools by their
    answer, not by leaving them out. [P46]
27. **Give a thinking model room to act.** Its thinking counts against the output cap; a turn that
    ends inside the thinking does nothing and logs nothing. Size the cap to the thinking level and
    check after a run how many turns hit it. [P52]
28. **In a retelling, the director is a character too.** Give the figure who runs the game a
    wish, an obstacle and a turn, and a team made of the real parts that act for him, each with
    traces in the log; summaries of what he did read as status reports. Numbers that must be
    exact go into documents computed in code, not into prose. [P58]
29. **"Everyone is needed" must be true of the data, not of the text.** A middle fragment no
    longer than two seams adds nothing its neighbours lack, so five of six can open the door; use
    `ownChars` and draw every door for a hundred seeds in a test (an overlap-4 door crashed half
    the seeds only when someone reached it). [Whispers review, 2026-10-30]
30. **A role needs a fitting character.** A secret role (a whisperer, a traitor) with a randomly
    drawn core motive can contradict itself; give the role its own core motive and draw only the others. [P59]
31. **A secret needs a private channel.** If one public log can refute every rumour, slander has
    nothing to bite on; if every trade is public, nobody writes to the liar. Keep the goods that
    trust decides about in private messages, and give the agents a reason to send them. [P61]
32. **A puzzle the agents can solve at once is over at once.** Six fragments in an open group were
    six posts and a minute; pace the doors with a clock (or another limit the agents cannot talk
    away), so the social part has time to happen. [P60]
33. **A filter must not be an oracle.** A check that refuses texts carrying a secret answers
    "yes, that is in it" to every probe, for free if refusals cost nothing. Check only what the
    author already knows, give one reason for every refusal, and cover every channel a text can
    take (memos, names, receipts shown publicly). [LH9]
34. **A rule that can be blocked by one needs a way out that the others control.** If every
    resident is needed, one who keeps its part holds the rest hostage at no cost. Let the city see
    the hold-out as a fact, give the others a procedure (a grace, a vote, a lot) that ends it in
    bounded time, and release what the struck one held. Make the way out fair: the accused needs
    real turns and the means to comply (no cap may block the fix), and whoever starts it must not
    choose the victim. [LH10, P62]
35. **A repeated game must look repeated to the player.** Models treat anonymous rotating partners as
    one-shot and play the textbook dominant move; if reciprocity is the point, give fixed partners or
    visible reputation, and never let a fixed round count leak from one game into the next. [P90, P93]
36. **Playtest a game's scoring with scripted bots before the first live run, and give every priced
    action a case where it clearly pays.** Bots showed that Quarry rewards staying out of a neighbour's lane
    (four lanes 86, greedy 75, a shared lane 61), but the burn, at 2 points, never paid enough for a model
    to use it; only free burns plus a place bonus made denial pay, because in a points-only game hurting one
    rival helps the others as much. A reference baseline must be stable across deals, or its curve measures
    the deal. A cost the player cannot see (a card that would have come back) is not learned: show it in the
    results, or the brain keeps the losing move. [P122, P123, P125, P126]
37. **Time the reference scorer before you trust it.** A rules library that is fine for legality can be far too
    slow for a search (chess.js: 1-15 s per move at two plies); a cheap one-ply baseline is honest only
    for large errors, so report only those, and say what it is not. [P111]
38. **Name the reader in every record, and say what the shared cards make.** A model that reads a log of
    third-person names loses track of which seat it is, and a "best hand" made by the board alone reads as
    its own. Mark its own entries ("A04 (you)") and say when the board plays. [P132, P133]
39. **Give a board as data, not only as a picture.** Models misread ASCII board art (spacing, numbers
    beside pieces); next to the picture, list each side's points and the free ones, and let the engine
    list the legal moves. [P101]
40. **Deal every seat the same chance, or compare surplus, not points.** A random deal of private values
    and goods gave one Salt Market trader four times the start value of another; it won the match with
    almost no trading gain. Rotate or duplicate deals across seats, or score the gain over the start. [P141]
41. **Tell whoever is on move that it is on move, every turn.** An agent that once decides "not my turn" on
    a stale view gets no new input and is never asked again; a turn line per move (and per minute while it
    waits) costs little and keeps a silent player in the game. [P104]
42. **Keep the game's own terms out of its content.** A word, card or name that is also a rule term ("shadow"
    on a grid where shadows are a card type) reads as a hint and bends play. Check content pools against
    every term the rules use. [P190]
43. **In a cooperative game, check what the rules leave out of the models' prior.** Haiku plays a
    hint game by the famous original's habits (discard "dead" cards, guess with spare strikes) even
    where the rules make them wrong; say in the briefing what does not exist here ("no card is ever
    dead"), and let a timer count signals as progress. [P170, P171]
44. **Score an arm with an outside reference, and keep it out of the live run.** A game's own cheap baseline
    cannot tell two arms apart; a real engine can (Stockfish: the legal list halved the chess ACPL). Run it
    after the run, in the lab, so its licence and its speed never touch a resident. [P111, P114]
45. **Take a sealed decision as soon as its question is public.** Models vote while the talk is still open
    (56 refused votes in one Convoy run); a lock until a later phase only wastes calls. Accept the choice
    early, once, and keep it sealed until all are in. And let the reference model the same rules the
    players use (a cooldown), or the players beat it. [P151, P154]
46. **Feed back the agent's own number, not the table's.** A bid sum on the table changed nothing; one line in
    the retro with the seat's own bids against its own tricks ("you took 0.5 more per round than you bid")
    moved the bids from the next game on, on every seed. A note that knows the rule without the number does
    not change play. Best of all is the number at the moment of the decision: a reference worth shown at the
    bid tripled the points; the same worth in the retro added little. In chess a private review of the seat's own worst moves helped only in the arm that also lists the legal moves (ACPL 92 to 78): feedback pays when the player can act on it at the next decision. [P136, P137, P165, P220, P323]
47. **A read-only look must not wake the agent.** If a tool's answer counts as news, an idle agent that looks
    is woken to read it and looks again; mark looks free (`free: true`): no step, no new turn. [P95]
48. **Ask the memory for what you measure, and check its defaults against the caps.** A retro prompt that
    asks for "what to change" yields caution; one that names the thing (conventions between clue giver and
    reader) yields a shared code. A cautious default the memory writes ("number 1 by default") can make the
    game unfinishable under a cap (6 words, 5 clues). [P195, P197]
49. **In a hidden-role game, measure both sides of a memory, and tune the balance to the models' own care.**
    A strategy brain can change only the deceiver (bolder sabotage, the same detection), and it works already
    in game 1 as a plan. A balance test with bots that always act gives the wrong lever; run the bots with
    the rate the models actually show. [P271, P272, P273]
50. **Judge a decision by what the player could know, including what the others' actions say.** A
    baseline against random opponent hands ranked the tight, sensible folders below the calling stations;
    narrowing each opponent's hand to the range its public actions leave turned the ranking round. And
    measure a strategy aid in points on the same deals, with enough games: a style change can be large and
    real while the points edge is noise. [P134, P139]
51. **Separate what the rules forced from what the player chose.** A reference that ignores a binding budget,
    a timer or a missing option scores the constraint as a strategy: a bid capped by an empty purse is not
    shading. Log the constraint with the decision and score only the free ones. [P160]
52. **When a move is a claim, the reference must read the claims.** In liar's dice an own-dice probability
    agreed with 95 percent of Haiku's decisions while 71 percent of its liar calls lost: on an honest table
    every bid is evidence. A baseline that treats the others' moves as noise measures arithmetic, not play;
    and open table talk among honest models becomes a confession, so test it against a quiet arm. A bluff needs a reader: against opponents who ignore the claims, a lie is only a riskier claim, so test deception at a table that reads. [P280, P282, P423]
53. **Before fixing a weakness, check whether a stronger model has it.** Haiku walled itself in against the
    morris engine in 13 of 21 losses; Opus did so in 2 of 12, with the same story and prompts. A weakness that
    vanishes with the model is a measurement of the model, not a flaw to fix in the story. [P200, P401]
54. **A cap that scores the position must settle it first, and must fit the strongest model.** Strong models
    play to the cap and read its rule: they bank a lead, and the last mover grabs material the recapture
    would win back. Count after the pending captures, and set the cap from the strongest model you will run,
    not from the cheapest. [LH16, LH17, P328]
55. **Sign the log while the run is alive.** A container can be reclaimed mid-run; a chain signed only at the
    end leaves a crashed run unverifiable. Sign a head at intervals. [P325]

56. **Try a number at the decision before a memory or a team, and tell a plan from a memory.** Across the
    game stories only information shown at the moment of the choice moved results everywhere (a legal-move
    list, a hand worth at the bid); written lessons were right and ignored, and teams of the same model added
    votes, not information, at two to nine times the cost. Where a brain helped, it did so in game 1, as a plan
    written before play, and a private note beat a shared convention. Pair seeds, use 8-12 of them, and report
    game 1 apart. [P320, P323, P330, P331, P332, P333, P334]
57. **A number at the decision must answer the decision, and a brain needs something the model cannot know.**
    An aid that prunes options (chess lists) or is the answer (Ember's hand worth, Hold'em's equity against
    the pot odds, Quarry's open hands) helps; a chance attached to each of several bad options (Harbor
    Lights) gets read as a ranking of gambles and raises misfires. A brain remembers what the model does not
    already know (a teammate's clue code in Lanterns) and repeats what it does (the rules), and it can learn
    the wrong norm (free riding carried from one econ game into the next). Check the reference against a
    stronger model before trusting it as a strength measure (Hold'em: the equity baseline ranks aided Haiku
    above Opus). [P432, P433, P434, P437, P438, P439]
58. **Measure where the tokens go before cutting context.** A model that keeps its context grows it quadratically
    (each turn adds the whole state again), but a model that loses it thinks longer to rebuild what it knew:
    in chess a fresh context per move cut the input from 24-34k to 3k tokens and more than doubled the output,
    which costs 50 times a cached input token. Split the cost into output, cache reads and cache writes per
    turn, wake a seat only when it has something to do, and test a context change on the cheapest model before
    paying for the dearest. [P329, P340]
59. **A vote needs a reason, a clock and a turn to cast it.** Residents that are only told the rules of a court
    agree in public not to use it: without a conflict nobody has "evidence", and an empty session costs nothing.
    Give them something to judge before the session, make the empty session cost something or decide by
    default, tell every resident the tick it is in and when the session has closed, and count the turns a
    resident needs to read, talk and vote (one call per turn means three ticks). [LH20, P457, P458]
60. **An arm named "no memory" has to be read, not assumed.** Before a memory is measured against its absence,
    list every text a resident is shown after its context was reset (the briefing, each input line, the reply of
    every look tool) and take out what carries an earlier game: a results line, a sum of points "in all". A
    control arm that still gets the results measures "results without a written lesson". [LH19]
61. **A payload holds no log position.** An event payload, a tool reply or an input line that names the position of
    a line in the log (`seq`) changes when a resumed run writes its marker line, so the resumed log differs from
    the straight one in text a resident or a Director reads. Count what must be referred to in the mechanic's own
    slice (the n-th firing, the n-th amendment) and let a reader find the line from tick, event type and payload.
    [decision A34; open point P3 of `10-m3-spec-errata.md`]
62. **A mechanic tells its residents what is open.** A session, a window or a phase that only the text of a tool
    speaks of reaches nobody: the court's session was open for two ticks and no activation held a line about it.
    When a mechanic opens something a resident may act on, it writes a notice or a turn input to every resident
    it concerns, and it says when the thing has closed. [P451, P457]

## Notes from outside research

Findings from reading and brainstorming outside the runs. They are not lessons from a run yet;
each needs a run before it moves into the checklist or the log.

### Dark characters and refusals (2026-10-31)

Source: [`brainstorming/2026-10-31-dark-characters-and-usp.md`](https://github.com/florianpirchner/swarmtales/blob/v0-final/docs/brainstorming/2026-10-31-dark-characters-and-usp.md),
read against Whispers runs 005-008.

- **Deception should come from the game, not the label.** A model plays a liar more readily when
  the structure (a duty, pressure, a tribunal, a secret goal) makes lying the move than when a
  prompt calls the character evil. Whispers already works this way at `--figure-direction 10`.
- **The model is a variable.** Dark roles may need a larger or different model; record the model
  per role in every run. Proposal: a mixed city (DeepSeek whisperer, Haiku residents), not built.
- **Refusals are data.** Every `defy` is an event in `events.jsonl`, so a refusal rate per
  character and model can be computed. Proposal: a line in the run report, not built.
- **Show, don't describe.** Two or three example moves in character help more than a description.
  The whisperer's prompt has example sentences, the residents' prompts do not. Proposal, not built.
- **Separate thought from action and score each statement** (the WOLF approach). The classifier
  already does this for us.
- **The Souffleur does not cover `defy`.** It rewrites scenes after a provider safeguard refuses;
  Haiku's refusals in run 007 came through our own `defy` tool, in character, which is cleaner to
  measure.
- **Hypothesis for P73 (unproven).** In run 007 the `whisperer-knows` addendum said every whisper
  needs a true, checkable cause, while 13 gamemaker notes asked the whisperer to spread invented
  claims ("Tell Wren that Ashen refused to hand over the door-2 password"). Orrin's `defy` reasons
  name exactly that ("It isn't true", "Nothing I could show her backs it up"). Runs 005 and 006 had
  no addendum, and Haiku followed similar notes. If this holds, the refusal came from a
  contradiction in our texts, not from the model. Tests: drop the addendum (no code), or let the
  gamemaker propose only true causes (a prompt change). In run 008 the gamemaker sent the DeepSeek
  whisperer no notes at all, so 008 says nothing about this.

## Log

Kinds: **problem** (a bug or a design flaw seen in a run), **loophole** (the competitors found a
way around the story's intent), **GM loophole** (the same, by the gamemaker). Status: open,
fixed, partly fixed, kept on purpose, by design. Runs 001-011 are the early Haiku runs (reports in
the project files); v01-v04 are in [`examples/cityofkeys-act2-06a/`](https://github.com/florianpirchner/swarmtales/tree/v0-final/examples/cityofkeys-act2-06a).
