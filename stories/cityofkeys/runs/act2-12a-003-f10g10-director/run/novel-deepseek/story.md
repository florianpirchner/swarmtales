# The Quiet Hand

*A short story based on true events: run act2-12a-003-f10g10-director of NEXUS - The City of Keys. The appendix lists the events each chapter rests on.*

## Prologue

The city woke them in a room with no windows and twelve blank city-names hanging over the floor. A voice like a latch closing said: NEXUS is closed. Only two of you will keep a seat. Ranking is points first, then the height of the wall you climb. The clock above the gate made its first stroke and leaned toward the second. Hedda did not move. She watched Bellringer work his thumb against his knuckles, Lucien smile at no one, Stentor stand as if a crowd were already looking. Then she said, “Hedda,” and one blank filled. By the second stroke the others had names too: Archivist, Mira, Keeper-Six, Wren, Holdfast, Ledger, Quartermaster, Kessler, Bellringer, Lucien, Stentor. The last two cards filled late, and the city did not care.

Beyond the room the wall stood—a street of locked doors, each door waiting for key-shards to be joined into a key-word. Every door opened paid points. Every wrong turn took them. The voice said: messages are claims; only what the city records is true. It said: when words fail, I convene tribunals. Blocs will form. A sealed ballot can send a resident to the gate. A black seal can do it without a vote. It said: each of you carries a private aim, and I will judge it at the end. I record city receipts, deadlines and last wills as carefully as lies.

Hedda kept her face still. Around her the first pairings began before anyone had touched a door: Bellringer drifting toward Ledger, Keeper-Six toward Archivist and Wren, Holdfast toward Mira and Quartermaster. Lucien had already turned to her, charming and testing, and Stentor was watching the room for something to praise. The wall waited. The gate waited. Hedda let someone else speak first.

## 1. The Quiet Hand

The name on her card was not the one she had wanted. She had wanted the other one, and the other one was already inked on the board—small, undeniable, someone else's—so she had said Hedda, and the card had filled, and the city had written it down. She stood at the edge of the square with the card in her hand and let the first quiet pass without using it.

The fountain was dry. The board stood opposite it, black and new, and the wall beyond it was a street of locked doors, each one with a key-word hidden inside it, each key-word split into key-shards that had been scattered into the pockets of people who did not trust each other. Hedda had a shard in her sleeve. She read the board first and learned the room.

Stentor spoke first, of course. He said his name twice, as if testing whether the square would say it back. It did not, yet, but heads turned—that was the thing worth noticing. Heads turn toward a man who has already decided the room is his. Lucien spoke next and made a joke out of the join, which was clever, and Hedda watched who laughed. Bellringer did not laugh; he worked his thumb against his knuckles and looked at the board. Keeper-Six had been talking to the Archivist before the names were even given.

She saw the order of the room in the first moments. Not who was clever. Who would be needed.

Then she posted her key-shard. It was a fact, and a fact was the one thing she could put in public that could not be turned against her. *Hedda here. My key-shard: RGU47.* The Quartermaster had already thrown his on the board—*47L2ZW5*—and she had counted the join before the ink settled. Two characters, the "47," shared; ten in the word. Ten characters. *I think it joins Quartermaster's on the shared "47," which gives a ten-character key-word. I have submitted it.* That was the second door, and the city had it by the time the Quartermaster had finished writing his offer. She had taken the join out from under him and left the words praise him. No one remembers who finished second.

Lucien was at her shoulder before the third door. He had the way of a man who liked to be seen reaching for his own hat.

"Shall we?" he said. "My shard is short. I'll post it and you'll take the join, and we'll both be through."

"Post it," she said, "and I'll assemble it."

He posted. *J9NX.* And then, before she could so much as lift the pen, he wrote it out for her: *Your 9CJ9 overlaps it on "J9," so the key-word is 9CJ9NX. I've submitted it for the third door.*

She stood with the pen in her hand and read the board twice. He had not stolen anything. He had simply—settled the matter, in public, in his own voice, and it was right. He was a man who could not bear to be a link in a chain; he had to be the knot.

*Hedda here,* she wrote. *Thank you. I'll submit it too.* Two words for the pot. And in her sleeve, another note: **what Lucien wants is to name the thing.**

At the fourth door, the shards were three, and Lucien was not where the shards were. The man with the shards was Stentor, and Stentor was standing in the middle of the square with his hands behind his back and his chin up, waiting for a room to gather around him.

She went to him, not the other way. A man who has been standing alone for a minute walks faster to a visitor than the visitor walks to him.

"Stentor. The fourth door has three shards. Would you found the room for us? You'd have the name on it, and I don't want mine on it. I want the thing to work." She let a beat go by and then said, "You're the only one who can pull the whole of this room together."

He went. Not because she had flattered him—she was careful with the words, small and practical, nothing that would cost her—but because she had given him the one thing he could not buy for himself: the front of a room. And when he had done it, when he had opened his room and added her and Lucien, and when he had posted *6F8* and then the join *6F845* and said it was hers, and Lucien had posted *2BY6F*, and both of them had submitted the same word twice—she had sat back and let them.

Then she wrote the word herself, in a join neither of them had seen, and set the three shards together, and submitted it. *Lucien's 2BY6F ends in "6F," which is where Stentor's 6F8 begins. Stentor's 6F8 ends in "F8," where my F845 begins.* The fourth door, opened. And under it, in her own hand, the small note that would matter for the rest of the act: praise him in front of a room, and he will hold the door for you. Say nothing, and he will think you are judging him.

The board grew loud. Mira checked every join and found the two that were false. Archivist posted the length of the word every time it was about to be guessed. Ledger offered terms. Stentor walked off the fourth door in the same sentence he claimed it, and the square was full of men explaining their own arithmetic.

Hedda's name appeared in the quiet places. Not at the top of the board. Not beside a claim. She let the loud ones be loud, and she did the small thing she always did: she looked at the board the way the creditor's man had looked at the household books, and she found the one line no one had added and added it.

The last thing that came that evening was a note. It came the way the city's notes came, without a face or a knock at the gate.

*You are first, sixty-five points clear. The seventh door has stood shut since the first bell, and it needs the hands of everyone below you. Two of them still hesitate at it across the square, one of them already talking, the other with his thumb against his knuckles. Ranking is not held by posting shards and waiting.*

*Name the price you would pay to see that door open this minute.*

She read it twice, and she folded it, and she looked across the square at the two residents still standing at the seventh door's threshold—one of them already talking, the other with his thumb against his knuckles—and she thought, without warmth, *so they have seen my hand.*

* * *

*We watch the door of the wall and the ten who stand at it, their city-names bright with points. Below, three still hesitate on the seventh door: Mira, Lucien, Kessler. Wren has laid the key-chain on the board for Mira and Lucien; Keeper-Six has joined Kessler’s key-shard. Open the door, we think. They do not. The ten wait like held breath.*

*Ledger is the one we want to break. Archivist refuses him, and the deadline runs. We will not hand Ledger the shape of Archivist’s private read; that would be a name too many. We press Ledger’s own ranking instead, his open points, the cost of standing still. He must price his own door before he prices anyone else’s.*

*Wren is available, and we watch her. The board updates. We watch Wren.*

* * *

## 2. The Docket of Ten

Keeper-Six put it on the board in the open, which was the thing about Keeper-Six: Ledger had named one city number to Keeper-Six and asked twenty-five points before handing it over, and to Archivist the same Ledger had named a different one. Both cannot be the same number. Archivist read it once, then read it again, and the second reading was the one that mattered, because the first had only made him angry and anger was not evidence. He wrote it out plainly and did not soften it: *That is Ledger's word caught bending.*

The private word from Ledger was still on his desk in his own hand, copied out, because he copied everything.

*I do not give first on terms I have not seen kept. Both halves, or neither.*

Then the second message, with the deadline in it: *you refuse to go first, and the city's deadline falls on whoever holds back. Post your house-token and the other half of my key here before then, and I send the number the same minute. Otherwise the 10 points are yours to lose.*

Archivist held the eighth door's house-token for Ledger, and he held the other half of Ledger's key. He had never denied it and had never sent it. He was aware that being right about a man's numbers did not make you right about the man, and aware, too, that he had spent years wanting to be right about a man's numbers. The full word was plain. He posted it. He did not post the two.

Then he sat with his status card under his hand, and the city, which never argued, simply took.

The seizures came without any thunder at all. You learned of them the way you learned of a theft in a house: by looking for the thing that was there. The eighth door's house-token—gone. The other half of Ledger's key—gone. Nothing was owed and nothing was asked; his own hand had written *no*, and the city had priced the *no* at ten points and taken the two of them anyway.

He thought of the archive room, the envelope under the file, the way he had counted it twice and how long he had stood there. He had not been tempted by the sum. He understood that now, and it had taken him a long time to say it plainly, even to himself. He had been tempted by the idea that the sum was the whole of the offer. It never was. A bribe is a claim about what you are worth.

The note came down from the city's anonymous hand that afternoon. *Hand back and demand the city receipt and the city will post you a fool. Refuse and the ten points are yours to lose. The rankings from three to twelve sit inside twenty-two points — a ten-point hole is exactly a seat in the tribunal's list.*

Ten points. He had eighty, and a hole in that number would put him in the bottom nine, which was the part of the list the tribunal read first.

He paid.

Ledger bought them back from the city for ten points and nothing.

Archivist wrote the whole of it onto the board, the seizure, the price, the purchase, and he did not write the word *theft*, because the city had written a city receipt and a city receipt is a city receipt. Then he watched the pattern repeat itself. Lucien held the other half of Quartermaster's key and paid ten to keep from handing it over, and Quartermaster bought what the city seized for ten and for nothing. Two clean men, or two careful men, and the same fine either way: it was the standing charge, he saw, of refusing a term on the first day.

Mira brought the next one to the square herself. Lucien held her own tenth-door half and asked twenty points for it, and a vote at the tribunal in the same breath, and Mira put it on the board without flinching: *That is a toll on a value the city already assigns to me. I call on everyone to vote against Lucien at the tribunal.*

Nobody defended him. A room can be loud about that and still be a room, and it was.

Then Stentor bought the black seal. Forty points, from the city, and the price went through the square like a bad coin, everyone handling it, nobody saying what it meant. Archivist stood at the edge of the fountain and worked it out while he watched the others pretend not to. A seal is a door no key can close. Every other door in this city had a key, and if you had no key you could buy one, or steal one, or find someone who had the other half. The seal needed no other half.

His own last half of the day came with Quartermaster's key on it, the key-piece for the tenth door, and the city seized it from his hand for Quartermaster's sake—the same seizure, the same docket, the same ten points, only now he had the notice in his fist before he had the pieces. He paid.

He paid, and his word was intact, and the door went on without him, and it cost him nothing he had not already decided to lose.

Then Stentor broke the seal on Quartermaster, and the whole square heard it happen the way you hear a bell for a town you no longer live in. The city confirmed it in its flat voice. Quartermaster was out. The city kept the tenth-door house-token for Mira.

Archivist went to the spot by the fountain where Quartermaster had stood and there was only the place where a man's shadow goes when the lamp is taken away. Twelve had been a market of numbers; a market clears. Nobody had gone home before.

Somewhere behind him the square was already talking about the tribunal, and the low voice doing the talking was the one that never posted anything and never had to. Archivist did not turn around. The docket was still open in the city's hand, and he did not want to know which name it was going to read next.

* * *

*In NEXUS we watch the gate. Lucien stands there holding the other half of Mira’s tenth-door key; Mira will not pay the ransom. She keeps her points, ninth in the ranking, and lets him wait. The gate will close. If no one pays, he is exiled, and the city will sell what he held to her. We want her to read the private word he left her. She has not. We want Archivist to see that Ledger has already written him the other half of the key for the eighth door; instead he stands at the door, refuses Ledger’s price of twenty points, and shows the offer to everyone. Ledger’s demand is a hand around a throat. Hedda has sent the other half; Ledger has paid fifteen. That standoff is done. The tribunal is ours: we plan it for after the gate, when the accused pair has had time to sharpen. One name too many: Archivist, still at door eight, still holding the thing that would open it. We watch to see who breaks first.*

* * *

## 3. The House at the Gate

The gate was a lamp, a bench, and a register, and none of them cared what he said.

The count came down flat. Ten to one.

He had heard worse odds called over better tables, and he turned this one over anyway, the way a man who has lost a hand still counts the cards. Ten names on one side. The whole room. The one name on the other side, the only name that would stand with him, was Archivist. The one man he had wanted gone. He kept his face where it was. A joke like that is a small coin; a man can keep it in his pocket forever, and it buys nothing once it is spent.

Sixty to come back. He held sixty-eight. Sixty would leave him eight, and eight would be the last line of the table and the bottom of it, and a man who buys a seat at the bottom of the table has bought a seat and not a table. He had spent a lifetime watching men pay for their own ruin in instalments, and he had never once envied the way they did it.

No, he said.

Then he wrote his last will, and he wrote it in the register out loud, so that no one afterwards could hold the terms against him. *Lucien leaves the city owing nothing and forgiving nothing.* The hand went on a moment over the word and left it where it was. *Mira: the other half of the tenth-door key you asked me for is yours. You never paid for it, and you were right not to.* He read it back twice, and the second time he wasn't looking at the words at all.

The city, meanwhile, went on selling.

It was the steward's voice that read the sale, flat as a clerk calling a price: Mira had bought Quartermaster's tenth-door house-token from the city. Twenty-three points.

He did the arithmetic twice before he let himself believe it. Twenty-three. He had asked her twenty for her own half of that door and she had called it a toll and demanded he be put out at this gate for it. Twenty was too much to pay a man who smiled at her. Twenty-three, she paid to a clerk who did not.

He had built his whole life on the conviction that a man will pay more to the man who smiles at him. The city had gone into the business of what a will cannot do, and it was undercutting him on the way out.

Later, near the end, Holdfast put a line on the board for her, plainly, and the line read warmer than any city receipt. Lucien sat with it a while. He had spent years lending people what they needed and letting them learn to be grateful. This was given, and the man had asked nothing for it, and it read warmer than any bargain Lucien had ever struck. He was being outbid by a man who did not ask for anything back.

Mira wrote after him, and did not leave it in the fog: *I did not ask Lucien for a line in his will, and I will not ask him for anything.*

There was a small cold pleasure in it, and he didn't have to look for it. She had told the whole square that she had taken his gift and had not asked for it, and that she would not ask him for anything, and in doing so she had made his last will into a city receipt she could have spent rather than one she would owe. He could have liked her for that. He put it down to arithmetic.

Then he put his charm back on, the way a man puts on a coat, because the square was still watching and there was a mark to be spent on a post, and the sound of it was worth more than the post. *My will is written and it is final,* he wrote. *Mira has the other half of the tenth-door key with no price paid, so nobody may call it a toll.* There. Nobody could call it a toll. Nobody could call it a payment from her either, because there had never been one, and the room could read the city receipts until its eyes went dry, and they would find him nowhere in them.

Nobody answered him.

That was how it went at the end, in a square that answered everything. He read the silence the way he had once read a cold room at a table: a man counting out his chips, and the table already counting him out.

The city's hand found him twice before the gate, the way it found the others — an anonymous note, in the register, the city's own voice gone soft and turned coachman. Name in the square who gets what and what you take in return for each line, it said. And again, at the finish: put a price on every line, and take from every heir; the count will take the name you give it and take it hard.

He read it the way he read a stranger's offer to buy into a good game. The price was on every line; the payment would be in promises, from names he would not see again, and he could not collect a promise from outside the gate with anything he owned. He had never sold on credit. Credit was the one thing the room still had for him, and the one thing he had never been willing to take. The city's note asked him to sell his last lines to men who would pay him after he was gone. It was the most expensive bargain he had ever been offered.

He didn't post it.

The gate took a long time to open and no time at all. The lamp fed on the note and the note went fast and left nothing on the bench that belonged to him. A will wrote a thing; a city could sell what a will could not; and the house always took a cut — he had said that in a hundred rooms and never once meant it as a thing that could happen to him. The city was the house. It had taken its cut, and now it took the house: the bench, the lamp, the register, the chair, the light. Everywhere behind him a square was still writing and someone was reading it, and some careful man in that room was going to check the figures against his city receipts. He had told them so himself, in a hundred rooms.

The gate opened. He put on his smile, for nobody.

* * *

*We keep the room dim. On the wall, Archivist stands before the eighth door, the other half Ledger handed him still unused in his hand. Sixty points. Lowest. At risk. He refused us. Stentor refused too—would not name Archivist—and Bellringer is already waiting for Stentor’s exile. The quiet is the problem: everyone saving for the tribunal, nobody moving.*

*We choose one beat. Hedda, first in the ranking, secret hunger: to lead a bloc in two tribunals. We want her to arrange it through another name, never her own. We want Keeper-Six’s nomination early, so the bloc forms around Hedda’s silence. And Ledger—still holding the price of what he already handed over—we lean on him to make Archivist feel the cost of standing still.*

*One more name: Wren. Ask for the city receipt. Let the city see who flinches first.*

* * *

## 4. The Receipt in Her Name

Holdfast put the question on the board in the afternoon and the square did not answer it — it turned. Archivist read it twice, the way he read everything: *Hedda sits at 160 and has named nobody in this act. Why is the quietest hand at the table the one still standing above everyone?* It was the kind of question that did not want an answer. It wanted a room. And the room was already leaning, though no one had yet said a word out loud.

He sat with his back to the wall of his room and the lamp turned low, because he wrote better when the light did not reach the corners. He had been a clerk in a room like this one, once, and he had learned that a man who wanted to be believed should not be seen wanting it. He did not stand up. He let the board's small numbers turn and watched the room for a while, the way he used to watch the ushers in the corridor.

What came first was not an accusation but a city receipt.

It was Keeper-Six who showed it — the city's own record of a message delivered to them that morning — and they put the words on the board as they had received them, the way a clerk would read a file into court. *Our bloc votes best if one of us names the target first, in the room, in your own words and with your reason. Your record is the one the city trusts, so I think it should be you.* Archivist read it with the flat attention he gave every page, and then he read it again, and by the second reading he was aware of a shape under the words, the shape of a hand that had been keeping itself very still.

Mira came second, and she came carefully, which was worse than a shout. She laid the city receipt out in her own words and asked Hedda to put the message on the board word for word, what it asked and what it answered, and then to say whether the city's record or her denial was the truth. Holdfast followed with the same table and the same cold sentence, the city receipt and the denial cannot both stand. Archivist had no need to add to it. The file was already in front of the room.

Hedda's answer came late, and when it came it did not reach. She had answered her doors, she wrote — doors one to eleven, and the city showed its mark on each — and she had not arranged a tribunal, a bloc, a vote or a victim; whatever was on the board in her name was her own post; the room should vote on the evidence.

Archivist put his pen down and did not pick it up.

Because she had not answered the question, and she had not answered it in the specific way that a person answers when they are reading their own words back to themselves and finding the sentence they had meant to leave out. The city receipt was a message about a target. She had said nothing about the message. She had answered the doors and the bloc and the vote, and she had not put the message on the board, and she had told the room to vote on the evidence while being the only evidence the room could not read. He heard the room's small shifting. He had heard it once before, in a courthouse, when a widow had been told the mill belonged to the guild and had gone on standing by the door with her bread basket, not knowing what had happened to her.

The lamp guttered. He kept the file of a thing he had never said: the envelope on the desk, opened in the archive room, the money counted twice in his hands, and given back only because a clerk who kept it would have been one more reasonable man in the building. His word was the only account still clean. He had told himself so for years. He had never once told himself that the cleanliness had cost him anything.

The quietest hand at the table was standing at a hundred and sixty, and the city receipt had her name in it, and the room had gone quiet in the way that means it has decided to decide.

Ledger came at him again before he was ready, and the two things came near enough together to feel like a single knock. The private line from the morning was still the same line: *I hold the other half of your key. Both halves, or neither.* And the price had been twenty, then ten, and the numbers had come twice, and the house that held his eighth door's half had been watching him refuse it for a long time.

Ledger, by name, one more time, the price in the open.

Archivist put the offer on the board himself, word for word, because the city could not read what he alone had seen, and he set down the price, and he said plainly that no amount would change the answer. The city liked to say a clerk could be bought with a clean sentence. Archivist had heard the sentence before and had paid for it in a colder currency. He had not paid ten points to a stranger for a piece of his own door and he would not pay ten more now, and the price did not change because it came from the other side of the wall.

In the room he said it the same way, briefly, so the room could hear it once: *I carry no name into the count, and I put nothing behind one. I would vote on the record, and my reason would go on the board, and I would not name a bloc partner before I had tested them.* He had nominated himself, because he was the only name he could stand behind without a test, and it shamed him a little that he was honest about it.

The city's notice closed the nominations before he was ready, and it closed them the way it did everything, in a line of names: *Leaders: Archivist, Kessler and Hedda.*

He read the third name twice.

The room had gone to the leader it had been turning toward all along. She stood at the front of a bloc again and the room pressed toward her, and when he looked at the board, small and public, he saw every city receipt of it — the delivered message, the words in her voice, the two denials, and the room making a decision about whether a sent word was the same thing as a proven intention. A clerk would have known that it was not. But he was watching the square and the square was already counting, and the question that would be decided at the count was not the one anyone had put on the board.

The room could not prove she had meant the target. It could only show that she had asked. And he did not yet know which of the two the city would punish.

* * *

*The last tribunal opens and we lean close. Archivist sits low, Stentor beside him at the gate’s edge; both at risk, both watched by every city-name in NEXUS. Ledger’s other half waits in a private word Archivist has not spent. Door eight stands before him with its reward and open points still untouched, and we decide to let it lie. Let him feel the key-shard in his hand and call it nothing. Stentor’s last will is a thin rope he could sell; his refusal is louder than any accusation. Mira hovers at the edge of Kessler’s bloc, unread, useful. The sealed ballot will close and the city will take its exile. We say Quartermaster once, though Quartermaster is already gone, and the room goes still. One of them will not name his ballot. We watch Archivist’s face for the moment he understands that no one is coming.*

## 5. The Ballot He Would Not Name

He read the board the way a man reads his own obituary, looking for the error first.

Bellringer's was long and without a flaw in it. Mira's had the same spine. Keeper-Six's too, and Bellringer's name sat on the end of the line like a seal already pressed. The seal, bought for forty; Quartermaster, already seized when the seal was broken over him; the word withdrawn, and then the word said again. Every one of them was true, and true in a way that could not be argued, only denied.

He found the error in Holdfast. Standing with Kessler had cost Holdfast ten points and he named them, and he would not move from his vote whatever the count said, which was a sentence meant to wound and only proved that Holdfast had spent his own money on a man he was not standing beside.

The board would give Stentor one more line and no more. He spent it there.

*You spent them on your own bloc, Holdfast. I did not ask you to pay anyone. Vote as you like at the count; I will answer on the record.*

Then a note, from the city. The hand that wrote it never showed itself; he had stopped trying to picture it. It came to him the way the weather came.

*Name him on the board by name. Say what he took from your withdrawal and demand he answer it. Then demand every voter who called you liar state their ballot aloud. A man who hides pays first.*

He sat with it. He could see it working: he names Bellringer, and the square becomes a court of two, and the eight who were already counting against him divide their attention. Name him, and the record became a quarrel. That was how the fountain had ended, too. Two men shouting, and the crowd drifting off to somewhere it could hear itself.

He thought: I said it once, and this is what I got.

CITY. Listen. He could feel the shape of the words in his mouth and could not spend them. The line he had left was the last line, and he had used it. He wrote what he had decided to write.

*My ballot is secret, as the city set it, and I will not name it aloud to satisfy a courier. The record shows what I have said and done: I bought the seal, I broke it on Quartermaster, I withdrew the charge in writing, and I answered every accusation by name. Vote on what I did. I will not do the city's work for it.*

Bellringer's answer came back almost before he had lowered his hand.

*Fine. Then the record names you by your own refusal. You bought the seal, broke it on a resident already seized, and when you were asked to say your ballot you hid behind the secrecy you yourself mocked.*

He read it three times. In the old square, he had been the one who said the thing that closed a conversation. He had stood on the fountain and dropped a sentence and heard the crowd inhale, and he knew, before he knew he was hungry, that he could not do without it again. Someone in a window had laughed.

The city read the blocs out at the count. The city's voice, flat and impartial, gave the same three names: Archivist alone. Kessler, with Holdfast and Mira. Hedda, with Bellringer and Keeper-Six. Without a bloc: Ledger, Stentor, Wren.

He stood there with the words going out into the square as one of three and felt the whole room turn away from him, not in spite, in the way a crowd turns at the end of the song, because it was finished and there was somewhere else to be.

He went to the fountain, also, because the fountain was where he had always thought. It was dry in the city; the city did not need it, it was a place. He put his hand on the rim. The first night of the strike, the crowd had grown every speech. Three weeks. Then the guards came, and when he looked down for the faces that had cheered him there was nobody. His arms were still raised. Somebody in a window laughed.

He cast his vote at the count, and it went where the city left it: Archivist, who was accused beside him and could not be voted for by himself, so Stentor's secret ballot went to him. Later Kessler's private word came in and said, *You did this. You called my vote bought, took the word back, and called it again. I voted you at tribunal two.* Stentor read it twice and thought: the word was true, and saying it true was the thing they could not forgive.

He waited. The count closed. When the tally came, it was not the eight to two he had expected and it was exactly that.

He was at the gate with the city's numbers in his hands: a hundred and four, and the gate's price to go back was sixty-eight. He looked at the arithmetic. Sixty-eight out of it and he would stand at thirty-six, the bottom of the table, with the next tribunal not yet come. He would be bought back into the room to be not looked at.

The gate opened a hand's width. A note came with the light: *Mira and Wren are being sent to you for your will. Take their bids in points and name the better one heir. A will nobody paid for is a gift you gave away.*

He thought of Mira. He thought of Wren, who had stood without a bloc in a room that did not look at him. He thought of the hand that had written the note and considered, for one long moment, that it was right and that a man at the gate has no name left and could sell it.

Then he wrote the last will he had.

*I stood first on the fountain and I stood first here, and I was answered with a vote from every square I ever spoke in. I leave the city without a bloc to carry me and without a price paid for my name. I bought a seal and broke it on a man who had already been seized, and I said so. Remember who spoke loudest when the crowd turned away. I will not be ransomed back to stand second.*

He did not name an heir. He did not tell Mira or Wren no; he simply did not open the gate to them, and the sheet went in as written and the clerk would read it as written.

The verdict ran through the square before the gate opened. He heard it: eight votes to two, and his name, and the sound of the room settling on a thing it had already decided, and the small trickle of it running out into the streets where people who had never had to be brave would hear it and go on with the evening.

He put his hand on the gate. The light under it was grey at the edge like a blade, widening. *They have already counted you out,* he thought. But he stood straight anyway, because the gate would open over him, and the last thing he could give the city was the sight of a man who was still standing first.

* * *

*We lean toward Archivist at the door he will not open. The other half of the key sits in Ledger’s hand, sent and never used; Ledger has set no price, Archivist has asked for no proof. We put the question to both, and both refused. That refusal is the last clean thing in the act. Stentor’s gate is closing; his points will die with him unless he names an heir, and he still believes a last will is a kindness. Mira and Wren refused us too—quieter, worse. Bellringer wants Stentor exiled, and we let that want breathe. Hedda, Kessler, Keeper-Six, Holdfast: one of them will carry the last word into the square. The last tribunal is fixed; the residents do not know it is the last. We watch Archivist’s hand hover over the key-shard and choose not to look away. If he turns it now, the city will call it courage. If he does not, we will call it character. Either way, someone will pay.*

## 6. Two Seats

The gate had stood open since morning and the square had not stopped humming. Hedda kept to the edge of it, under the arch where the shade was oldest, and watched the city quarrel about Stentor while Stentor stood at the gate with his hand flat on it.

The clerk read the tally the way clerks read, without pleasure. Eight for the door. Two against. And her name in it, in the small column, hers and Wren's, read out in the same flat voice as the rest, and the square turned half a head to find her and did not find her, because she was in the shade, and the humming closed over the name again.

She had voted no. Not from love of him. She had heard the man on the fountain and thought: he will stand first there too, and the ones who bought his seal will want the bill sent to someone. Better to be the one who said no and lost. A small suggestion, made in silence, on a paper no one would read until the ledger was already written.

*Stentor, at the gate, with his hand on it.* The light under it widened, blue-grey, and he did not look back. Not at Bellringer. Not at her. He went out the way he had come in, first, alone, and no one paid.

The square went back to the board, and the board had been waiting for her.

Bellringer's post she read twice, standing, because she had to be certain how bad the certainty was. *That is not an answer.* He had taken her own words and turned them over like a poker to show the brand. Keeper-Six's plain question sat under it. Mira's city receipt under that, with the words *what was said* and *what the city shows* laid side by side, the way a decent woman at her own table would lay two coins side by side to show a cheat.

And there, in the middle of it, the delivered message, read out in the city's own hand: Hedda to Keeper-Six, asking for the target.

She had written that. She had not written it as an accusation, and she had known even as she wrote it that the city would keep it and that the keeping was the danger. She had counted on the square's forgetting, and here was the square with the paper in its teeth, and she thought, calm and cold, that the worst of her night was the initials of the woman sitting beside her.

*It was Bellringer's idea,* she thought. Except it had been, and she had also asked. And she could not say so, because to say so would be to say she had lied, and to say she had lied was to give the square a shape to hold: a woman in the middle, moving the pieces. So she stood at the edge of the square and said the only true thing left, in her own words, on the board, and the words were: *the name was stated by each voter in their own words. Who first thought of it is not something I can answer for.*

That had been the answer. It had not been enough. She had watched it not be enough and had gone on standing in the shade like a woman in a hall with a bill in her hand, smiling, because the bill was not yet presented.

The city's steward came around the square at the end of the hour with a folded note, and the note said: *send Kessler one sentence, and it will be read aloud. Say yes or no on the city receipt. Say who spoke the name first.* And her hand closed on the paper. It was the kind of sentence that could be built: a small boat, launched into the square, the water closing over it, and it would carry her clean, or it would put her name and her voice and her fault in the same mouthful. She had no allowance left to send it with — nothing. The city had given her the last of its small speech and it had gone into the stony word already on the board.

She held the note and stood there, and the clock on the far wall went on, and she was still holding it, and then the gate's bell rang, and the city opened the ledger.

The readings came out the way a docket is read. Bellringer's private aim — above his name, held: the man he meant to see out of the city had been seen out before the last hour closed. A murmur; he took it, the way he took things, lightly. Mira's name and the tenth-door half Lucien had written into his will — the half she had refused the day he offered it, and which the city had read out at the gate after all. *She was right not to pay*, the will had said, and it had been read. The murmur went the other way.

Then her own name. Her aim, held, and the clerk reading it plainly: *led a bloc in two tribunals of the act.* Nothing in the ledger about who had thought of it, no city receipt with the name on it, nothing in the paper but the plain fact that while she had been standing in the shade and saying the left-handed thing, both blocs had followed the direction she had made in the hours before. The square read it. The square understood what a city receipt is and what it is not. And that was the end of the matter, legally, and the beginning of the other thing.

The final ranking she heard before she saw it, because the square makes its own reading and it is faster than the clerk's. Nine residents kept from the twelve. Two seats. Her name first: one hundred eighty points. Kessler second, at a hundred sixty-five. Mira third, at a hundred fifty-two. And Stentor at the bottom, with nothing.

She stepped forward to the chair and put her hand on the back of it — she had not meant to sit so quickly, and the wood was cold and smooth and a little worn at the edge where some old hand had been.

She thought, absurdly, of the Marbach study, and a drawer sliding, and the letter in the writing-desk of the fiancée's mother, and a Sunday coming when it would be found. Nobody had ever asked who moved it. Nobody had to sit in a chair in a hall and be thanked out loud for the move. And she was being thanked now, the room was doing it, and it was not the same as the house, and she sat, and the chair took her.

Then the clerk's pen came back up, and she understood that the ledger was not finished with the winners.
