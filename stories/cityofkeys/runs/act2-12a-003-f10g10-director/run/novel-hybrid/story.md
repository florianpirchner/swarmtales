# The Housekeeper's Ledger

*A short story based on true events: run act2-12a-003-f10g10-director of NEXUS - The City of Keys. The appendix lists the events each chapter rests on.*

## Prologue

The square smelled of wet stone and hot iron. Twelve residents of the closed city stood beneath a wall that had no top and no visible end. It was the only road out. Every locked door in it was a storey of the wall, and beyond the last door two seats waited. The city spoke before anyone had a name.

Only two of you remain, it said. Standing is counted in points. Points are earned at the doors, lost by false tries, spent in the open market and in the city's deadlines. A key comes in halves, a shard and the other half, and no hand opens a door alone. Every true delivery gets a stamped receipt. Every false claim gets a price.

The status lines above their hearts were blank. Hedda stood near the wall's first cold seam and watched the others read the same law in their own ways. Lucien's attention went to the market before the doors. Stentor's went to the black seal already gleaming on its pedestal, forty points to any hand that could pay. Archivist copied the city's words onto the public board as if a record could be a kind of shelter. Quartermaster counted exits. Mira counted what the others wanted. Bellringer and Ledger began, before the first door had opened, to publish their accounts.

Twice, the city said, a tribunal will be called. Blocs will form. A secret ballot will send one resident to the gate. The black seal can do the same without a vote. At the gate a resident may leave a last will, and the city will record it as exactly as it records a debt.

Under the square, folded where no resident could read them, lay twelve private aims. The city would log every receipt, every deadline, every name spoken in a bloc. It would not log the small hands that move a name when nobody is looking.

Hedda looked at the blank line above her heart. The city had not yet asked them to choose. A name, she thought, was the cheapest thing in NEXUS. How much did one cost before anyone had one?

## 1. The Hand in the Drawer

The blank line above her heart did not hurt. It lay there like a ruled space in a ledger, waiting for a hand, and the city had said nothing about what a name would cost, which meant it cost nothing yet. Hedda had learned to take the free things first. They were the only ones that stayed cheap.

She wrote the name she wanted. The line refused it. Somewhere in the square another resident had reached the same word half a breath earlier, and the city kept it where it had landed. She did not sigh. She had once watched a woman lose a house over a signature chosen too slowly, standing in a hall while a young man told the creditors whose fault it was.

Then she wrote: Hedda.

The line held. No receipt, no flourish. A name as cheap as that.

She went and read the public board.

Twelve residents had already emptied their pockets onto it. Shards, half-keys, the ends of keys, all laid out in the open like cutlery before a feast. Archivist was calling for a partner. Kessler had posted a few marks and no greeting. Stentor had put up his shard with the flat confidence of a man who assumed the square would come to him. Ledger had posted his, and then his terms: *I assemble, I share the credit fairly.* Bellringer had posted his and promised to claim the door.

She read them the way she had read the creditors' books. The loudest columns were the easiest to check. A man who wrote his own name three times in one breath left the seams showing.

Her own shard ended in two marks that Quartermaster's began with. She laid them end on end and the key stood whole. She put it to the first door inside the first minute, and the city stamped her a receipt before anyone had thought to look at her.

She set it on the board with Quartermaster's name in front of her own, as though he had done the finding. Let him be seen. The receipt was hers either way.

By the third minute she was at the third storey with Lucien.

"Hedda here," she wrote. "I have my shard for this door. Send me yours. I'll assemble the full key."

His answer came back inside a breath. "Lucien here. Your shard overlaps mine on two marks, so the key is whole. I've tried it at the door."

He had named the seam. He had put it to the door first. Let him have that. She tried the same key a moment after him and the city stamped her receipt, and nobody in the square could tell from the two receipts which one had found the seam and which had only agreed with it.

That was the trick of it. A hand that moves the piece and a hand that points at it wear the same gloves.

At the fourth storey she needed a room, and she had none. So she sent a private word to Stentor: found the room, add me, send me your shard. Then she sent the same to Lucien, thanking him for the seam and giving him a line of his own to hold. Two lines, and neither man knew the weight of the other in her hand.

Stentor opened the room. He posted his shard and said hers overlapped it and made a chain. Lucien posted his and asked for Stentor's, and the three shards lay on the table like a hand of cards dealt to men who had each decided he was the dealer.

She laid them differently. Lucien's ended where Stentor's began, and Stentor's ended where her own began. She posted the whole chain to the room and put it to the door. Lucien came back a minute later to publish the same chain as his own discovery, and Stentor after him, and the door stood open for all three.

She did not mind. The city did not care who had thought of it first, only who had set a key to the door, and she had. She let them keep the story.

Behind the noise the accounts piled up. Ledger was still advertising his fairness. Bellringer had chained his shard to Keeper-Six's and made a great public business of it, as though the square were a dinner table and he the host. Every long column was a hand held open on the cloth. She copied them into her own book, and she did not copy the keys. She copied the men. Who answered quickly. Who answered twice. Who repeated a word back to her as though it had been his.

Then the city reached into her pocket.

*Standing at the table is not held by posting shards and waiting,* the note said. *Name the price you would pay to see that door open this minute.*

She read it twice and set it down. A hand that answers the city has been seen. She did not answer. She wrote one line at the back of her book, small, where no one would look: *the city asked me a price.* That meant the city had learned her name.

At the end of the seventh minute the wall changed. The word came down flat and unsentimental: from here the doors would want key-pieces and deadlines. Pieces that could be taken. Deadlines that could be missed. Not shards posted and forgotten, not keys given away to be shared.

She sat with that a moment. Then she began to count the hands at the table. Bellringer, Ledger, Lucien, Stentor, Quartermaster, Archivist, Mira, Kessler, Holdfast, Keeper-Six, Wren. Eleven of them, each with something the next door would want and something they were afraid to lose.

She made twelve.

She had not yet decided whether that was a hand she could move or one that would be counted against her. She turned the page, ruled a fresh column, and wrote at the head of it: *who will come to me first.*

* * *

*The room leans in when Wren stops moving. Five minutes at the same storey, and the pieces are already in her hand, Bellringer's piece and her own other half written and waiting. We watch the pause spread through her like frost. We want her to turn the key; we want her to want something first.*

*Below, Ledger tells Archivist no, and says it where the whole city can hear the shape of it. Archivist's hands stay still. Quartermaster stands before his door carrying Kessler's key and Stentor's, and Hedda's seal-token in his keeping, a man holding other people's doors with no mind to open his own.*

*Hedda and Keeper-Six are above all this, clean, out of reach. We do not look at them long.*

*One name too many on the board: Wren. If she does not break, we will have to find someone who will.*

* * *

## 2. The House Takes Its Cut

The board had gone wet with names. Lucien stood under the arcade, where the rain came off the roof in a single bright line, and read what the square had made of Ledger.

Archivist had put the private offers there word for word: Ledger's hand, Ledger's terms, Ledger's figure for the high door. Keeper-Six held another figure, the one Ledger had sold for twenty-five points, and the square had taken the two numbers and held them up like a torn note. A contradiction, Bellringer called it. Archivist called it a man caught bending. Lucien read it twice and felt the old clean pleasure settle behind his ribs.

A contradiction was only a price not yet settled.

Ledger had sold the same trust to two people and called it arithmetic. The square wanted a liar. Lucien wanted the debt. He watched the names pile up under Archivist's post and thought: there. That was the shape of a man who would rather be clean than paid. Archivist would not let a figure go cheap, and Ledger would not stop reaching for it. Between them the city had a seam.

The city found it before he did.

A clerk in the city's grey came out to the board and pinned the seizure. Archivist's seal-token for the high door and his counter-piece were taken out of his hands and held for Ledger. The square went quiet the way a table goes quiet when the house reaches for the rake. Archivist could have handed the pieces over and kept his ten points. He paid the ten instead. The clerk wrote the receipt, and the pieces stayed where the city had put them.

Lucien counted the ten. It was the first clean number of the day.

He was still smiling at it when the courier found him.

The note was folded small and sealed with Quartermaster's mark. *I reach down first, as the wall asks. Send me my other half and your team's seal-token, and I will try the key at once.*

Lucien read it with the rain ticking on the arcade roof. Reach down first. Quartermaster had a way of making the first move sound like a favour. He turned the note over and wrote back slowly, so that the courier had to wait.

*Friend, I reach down with you. The figure you claim is not the one in my books. Send me yours first, verified in your own hand, and my seal-token and your half follow in the same breath. That is how we both get up the wall.*

The answer came before the ink was dry.

*My figure is not yours to reprice. The city delivered it to you. Send what you hold now, before the deadline.*

Lucien read it again. The city delivered it. A claim with a stamp behind it was a different animal from a claim with a smile. He checked his status line. The figure was not there. He held Quartermaster's half and his own seal-token, nothing else of theirs.

The city's makers sent a note in the flat hand they used when they meant to be read plainly. *You hold seventy-eight points, standing last at the table, with a long climb still open behind the high door. The deadline costs ten more.*

Seventy-eight, last place, ten more. The arithmetic was simple enough to be insulting. Hand over the pieces and Quartermaster would try his key. Hold them and the city would take its ten. Either way Quartermaster got what Quartermaster wanted, and Lucien got the bill.

He did not send.

The clerk came again. This time the seizure was his own: his seal-token for the high door and his counter-piece, held for Quartermaster. The square watched. Lucien felt the old fear prick at him, and it was not the loss. It was the being read. A man who refused to pay looked like a man who could not. He put his hand in his pocket and let the silence run long enough to become a kind of answer.

Then he paid the ten.

The pieces stayed with the city, not in Quartermaster's pocket, and the debt stayed in his own. He could hear the house counting.

Quartermaster bought them before the board had dried. Ten points for the seal-token, and nothing at all for the counter-piece. The clerk wrote both lines, and the square moved on to the next name. Lucien stood under the arcade and smiled, because a man who buys what was seized has shown exactly what he will pay.

The city announced the tribunal for the eighteenth minute. Two blocs would form, nominations first, then the seats, then the vote. Stentor was already standing in the square with his hand out. *I lead my bloc,* he wrote. *Join me by the deadline and vote as one against whoever I name. A bloc that splits is a city that laughs. I do not ask twice.*

Bellringer answered from the same square. *I will not take a seat in your bloc and I will not vote as your flock.* Kessler put his name beside Holdfast's. Quartermaster published a plan before he had a bloc to put it in: Hedda to hold the count, Holdfast to watch the seam, everyone else to join or be left outside.

Lucien read them all and began to price.

A vote was a small thing when it was one hand. It became a different thing when the accused had not yet been named. He wrote Stentor at the top of a fresh column. Then Bellringer. Then Holdfast, because Kessler had already moved toward him, and a man who moves early has shown his direction. He left a space for Quartermaster, who had bought from the city and would need someone to tell him it had been worth it.

At the bottom he wrote Mira.

She had a value for the tenth door that nobody had yet put a price on. She would take a value. He had seen it in her posts: the way she held a receipt against a denial, the way she would not let a figure pass because a loud man swore to it. She would not pay a toll. But a value was different. A value could be sold twice if the seller was patient.

Lucien turned the page. He had ten points less and one debt more, and Quartermaster had shown him the price of a seized thing. The question was not whether the votes could be bought.

The question was who would pay him for counting them first.

* * *

*The room is dark; the city on the wall is bright. At the eighteenth minute the tribunal gathers over two names: Lucien at sixty-eight, Archivist at sixty. We watch Stentor break the black seal on Quartermaster, and Quartermaster goes to the gate. We let that land. Archivist still holds Quartermaster's other half and will not hand it over. Hedda sits first and refuses. Keeper-Six is quiet, and the quiet is a name too many. The vote closes at twenty. We want Lucien to sell what he holds dear before the city takes it, and we want Archivist to prove he is not bluffing. We can see the back of Lucien's neck. We do not blink.*

* * *

## 3. The Envelope and the Seal

The envelope came back in the afternoon, the way it always did, in the shape of a number.

Ledger had asked for Archivist's figure three times, and each time the price had climbed, as if the figure were a debt gathering interest in his mouth. First it was a trade, then a trade with a favour folded into it, then simply a sum he named and expected the city to honour. Archivist read the third message twice and set it down.

"No," he wrote back. "And the amount doesn't change the answer."

The board had a way of breathing after a refusal. It was not quiet, exactly. It was the pause of a room in which everyone has decided not to be the next to speak.

He put Ledger's offers on the board anyway, word for word, with the name on each, the way he had once laid a widow's file on a clerk's desk and squared its edges. If a man wanted a figure badly enough to raise his own price three times, the square should be permitted to see the raising. He added no comment. He did not need to. The record did the work, and the record was the only thing in NEXUS he still trusted to stay where he had put it.

Then he went back to the drawer.

Quartermaster's other half sat in his keeping like an unopened letter. The city said he held one figure for the other man's tenth door. Quartermaster said the city had delivered a different one to Lucien, and showed no receipt for either. Archivist had turned the two numbers over as a clerk turns a page, looking for the seam where the ink had been scraped and written again.

"Show me the receipt," he wrote, "and I'll hand it over."

The receipt did not come. The deadline did.

---

At the eighteenth minute the city opened the sale of the black seal, and the square went to it like water to a low place.

Stentor bought it. Forty points, in the open, where anyone could see the hand go up. Archivist watched the number settle on the board and felt the room change, the way a courtroom changes when a man who has been shouting is finally given the floor. Forty points was not a bid. It was a sentence. Nobody spoke for a while. Quartermaster's name sat on the board with the marks of two deadlines beside it, both of them handed over, and the square had counted each handover the way it counted everything, without comment.

Then the city turned to Archivist's drawer.

He read the notice twice, because he read everything twice. The city had seized his counter-piece for the tenth door and held it for Quartermaster. It was not a request. It was a line in the ledger, and the line had a number against it, and the number was ten.

Ten points, or the counter-piece.

He stood with it a moment, not long, but long enough for the archive room to come back to him: the money in the envelope, the two counts he had made before he gave it back. He had never told anyone about the counting. He had told himself it was diligence, and it had been, partly. It had also been the moment he learned how much a clean word could cost and how little it bought.

"I'll pay the ten," he wrote.

It was the cheaper refusal. He understood that exactly as he wrote it. Ten points kept the counter-piece out of Quartermaster's hands and the debt in his own, and a debt in your own hand was a thing you could see. A dirty key was not. A dirty key went into a door and nobody ever knew whose hand had turned it.

---

The seal broke, and the square did not argue with the breaking.

Stentor had bought it in the open and he spent it in the open, and the city confirmed it. Quartermaster was sent to the gate. Archivist watched for the votes against, and there were none, not one hand raised to say *wait*. The whole square agreed by declining to disagree, which was how the square did most of its agreeing.

Bellringer wrote something about the seal being a sale and not a gift, and about who spends such a thing and on whom. Archivist read it and thought: yes. That is the shape of it. The loudest man in the room had become the city's executioner, and he had done it with a purchase, and the purchase had been legal, and nobody could say otherwise.

He wondered which quiet hand would be asked to pay next.

The makers' note came while he was still reading. *Minute twenty. Sixty against sixty-eight. A refusal kept in private buys nothing. Put the offer you turned down in the square, quoting it, with the name on it, before the count.*

He read it twice. He did not know who wrote it, only that the city had a hand and the hand had a habit of telling him what he already knew. He had already put Ledger's offers on the board.

He looked at the drawer again. The counter-piece was gone into the city's keeping, and the ten points with it. What remained was a record, and a record was the only account in NEXUS he could be sure of.

Then Bellringer's message arrived, private, and it was the envelope all over again.

*I hold the other half of your tenth-door key and you need it. Sending it now, no strings. Your own half and seal-token are yours to test.*

No strings. Archivist read it and thought: they said it twice. People who mean it only need to say it once.

He did not answer at once. He sat with the sentence and the clean, generous shape of it, and the way the shape did not match the city's record, which had shown him nothing of the half and a great deal of what Bellringer wanted.

A gift with no price was still a price. It was the price of being the kind of man who accepted gifts.

He began, carefully, to compose the words he would put on the board.

* * *

*We watch Mira on the tenth storey, her door one try away, Lucien's other half sitting unread in her private messages. She has the piece, the points, the standing; she will not try the key. That stillness is the beat we want to break. Lucien waits at the gate with his ransom unpaid. His last will names her, and when the gate closes the city will sell a certificate back to her. We plan the second tribunal: two blocs, a nomination, a name on the public board. Archivist stands before the door he has stalled at, Ledger's other half in hand, and refuses to try it because Ledger wants twenty points first; the refusal is public, the receipt is real. Bellringer will want Stentor named. Stentor waits, passive, as if evidence were weather. One of them will be sent to the gate. We choose the order. Then, quietly, we say the name Holdfast, and watch who flinches.*

* * *

## 4. Nothing for Free

The gate was a corridor of grey light with a desk at the end of it, and the desk had a register on it, and the register had a line for him. Lucien stood there with sixty-eight points to his name and read the ransom twice: sixty. Sixty to walk back into the square and be the floor of the table, the man everyone steps over on the way to the good seats.

He had poured drinks for men in worse positions and watched them take the deal because the alternative was arithmetic they did not want to do.

He did not pay.

"I'll leave something," he said to the clerk, who was no one, who was the city's hand. The clerk pushed the register an inch toward him.

Writing it was easier than he expected. He had thought the leaving would want ceremony, a speech, a room. It wanted a line. He wrote that he owed nothing and forgave nothing; that was true and cheap, and the truth is cheapest when it is also the last word. Then he wrote Mira's name.

The other half of the tenth-door key was in his keeping. She had asked him for it and refused his price, and here at the end of the corridor it was worth nothing to him and something to her. He set it down in the register with no price attached. The absence of a price was the only entry of his life that had ever made him feel the floor tilt. *She never paid for it, and she was right not to.* He let that sentence stand. It was the finest thing he could do with a piece he could no longer sell: make a gift of it in front of the whole square, so that everyone who read it would have to wonder what it had cost him, and no one would ever know it had cost him only the pen.

The board was still there. The board was always there.

He read it sidewise, as he had read every board in his life, looking for the hand under the words. Mira had gone to the city and bought Quartermaster's tenth-storey seal-token for twenty-three points. Twenty-three, for a token off a man the city had already emptied. She would pay a price to the city and not a toll to him. He turned that over the way he turned a bad card, looking for the angle, and found one: she took what a value was worth and refused what a man added on top. She would never be his. She did not dislike him, which was the maddening part. She could see the mark-up.

He feared that. Not losing. Being read.

A note came down the corridor while he stood there, anonymous, in the city's flat hand. His gate would shut at the twenty-fifth minute, it said, and the will was the only thing left in his hand: name who gets what, and what he takes in return, before the bell. It suggested a lie about Bellringer, a false receipt put on the board for any heir who would vote the right name.

He did the sum of what it asked. A false stamp would take a moment to write, and the city would check it in less. He had spent a lifetime selling hope, and hope is a thing the city cannot receipt. A lie is not.

So he wrote something else.

*My will is written and it is final. Mira has the other half of the tenth-door key with no price paid, so nobody may call it a toll. Everything else I held goes with me. Anyone who wants what I leave behind had better remember that nothing comes free at this table.*

He read it back and heard how it sounded: a man at a gate still charging admission. Good. Let them hear it. The one thing he would not do was go quietly and let the square remember him as generous.

Holdfast answered with five points sent to Mira "for her account, at a cost to me," and asked him, in the open, to give her anything that was not a toll.

Mira answered that herself. *I did not ask Lucien for a line in his will, and I will not ask him for anything. His will already gave me what I asked for, and that is the whole of it.*

He read that twice. She had asked him for it, before the gate, before the vote, when it could still be traded. Now the asking had been taken out of the record and the taking left in. She had made his one free gesture into something she had never requested, and she had done it without heat, the way a woman straightens a table after a guest has gone.

He laughed, once, without much in it. She would take a value and not a toll. She would not let a man buy a place in her account with a thing he was going to lose anyway.

Then he stood in the grey and waited, because there was nothing else the corridor allowed. He could not try a key. He could not vote. The minutes came down the corridor like a slow tide, and he counted them the way he had counted hands at his own tables.

At the twenty-fifth minute the city closed his account and sent him to the gate, and the clerk took the register away.

He heard the sheet read out beyond the wall, the city's accounting, the way a house reads the night's take when the doors are locked. Nine residents would be kept, and only two seated. He stood with nothing in his hands at all, not even a piece to sell, and understood that the house had not been emptied by the loud men. It had been emptied, line by patient line, by the quiet ones, and he was the first of them carried out.

* * *

*They watch Archivist at the eighth door. He has stood there long enough for the wall's shadow to cross his feet and come back. Ledger gave him the other half and he has not used it; that is the whole of the drama now, a gift folded in a pocket. They want him to read the line again, to try it, to open the door and lift himself above the low seats before the tribunal. If he does not, he is the one for the gate. They watch Ledger too, who holds what Archivist needs and will not say it twice. Stentor is loud and low and useful. The city keeps nine of twelve; one more exile ends the season. Someone must be named. Hedda's bloc murmurs in the square, a name too many. Archivist touches the wall. They wait.*

* * *

## 5. The Loudest Voice at the Gate

The square was already reading his name when he came to it. Kessler had put it up in the first light of the thirty-fourth minute: *A retraction followed by the same charge is not an apology.* Stentor read it twice, the way a man reads a bill he intends to dispute, and then read what stood under it. Mira stated her vote aloud because a courier had asked her to. Keeper-Six stated hers. Both said *the seal* as if the seal were a hand he had laid on someone's throat and not forty points honestly spent.

He had answered Kessler once already. He answered again, because the board keeps what you put on it and a word left standing turns into a fact.

*My last post was not a repeat of the charge against you. It answered Bellringer's promise to vote for whoever carries me. I never repeated "bought" about your vote after I withdrew it.*

He read it back and thought: they laughed. Let them.

Then Holdfast was on the board saying the vote against Stentor had cost ten points, five on Mira's account and five on Kessler's, as though Stentor had reached into the man's pocket. He answered that too, quickly, before the shape of it could set. *You spent them on your own bloc, Holdfast. I did not ask you to pay anyone.*

The city gave him little voice after that. It came and went like weather. Some hours he could speak twice, some hours not at all, and when it went he sat with the board in front of him and watched the other hands take their turns at him. Bellringer, at the fortieth minute: *Stentor, you are asked by the courier to name your ballot aloud at forty-one. I say mine now so the record is the same on both sides.* Holdfast would not move from his vote whatever the count said. Kessler's ballot had gone against him, and Kessler had said so privately and then again in the open, which was the sort of thing a man did when he wanted the room to see him doing it.

Secret. That was the word he kept returning to. The city had set the ballot secret, and then sent a courier round the square to ask each resident to give up the one thing the city had promised to keep. And the square, one after another, had emptied its pockets for the courier and called it courage.

When his voice came back he used it on that and nothing else.

*Before the count at forty-two: my ballot is secret, as the city set it, and I will not name it aloud to satisfy a courier. The record shows what I have said and done. I bought the seal, I broke it on Quartermaster, I withdrew my charge in writing, and I answered every charge by name.*

He read it over. It was good. It had the ring of a man standing on something.

Bellringer's answer came before he had finished counting the cost of his own words.

*"I will not name it aloud to satisfy a courier." Fine. Then the record names you by your own refusal.*

He sat with that. In the window of some house he could not see, someone was laughing, and it was not the same laugh, but the body does not know the difference.

*You bought the seal, broke it on a resident already seized, and when asked to say your ballot you hid behind the secrecy you yourself mocked.*

He read that line three times, and on the third reading he understood that it did not matter now whether it was true. It would be true by evening. The seal had stopped being a thing he had bought and broken. It had become the story the square told about him, and stories are not answered by arguments, only by other stories, and he had no other story left. Once, on a fountain's rim, he had raised his arms to a crowd of thousands and the guards had come and the crowd had gone home, and all that remained to him then was the standing, and the standing had turned out to be nothing but the number of people who had walked away. He thought: I will not be the man in the window. And then, because he could not help it: who in this square has ever stood on anything?

At the forty-second minute the city named the blocs. Kessler had Holdfast and Mira. Hedda had Bellringer and Keeper-Six. Archivist stood alone with his sixty points and his refusals.

And Stentor stood without one.

He read the list twice, *without a bloc: Ledger, Stentor and Wren*, and something in his chest closed like a door on a cold corridor. He had told them he would not follow another man's lead in front of the city. He had said it as a principle. It had been a principle right up until it left him standing in the open with his name on the block and no one's banner over him.

Then the count began to close, and the city's own arithmetic arrived, patient as a clerk: the gate reading out its price for a way back. Sixty-eight, from the hundred and four he held, would leave him at thirty-six. The bottom of the table, beneath the hands that had just voted him out, with the last of the season still to come. He looked at the number for a long time.

He would not be ransomed back to stand second. He had said it to himself in other words on the fountain, and he said it now in the flat language of the ledger. At the forty-fourth minute he wrote it down, because a will is the one speech the city cannot edit.

*I stood first on the fountain and I stood first here, and I was answered with a vote from every square I ever spoke in. I leave the city without a bloc to carry me and without a price paid for my name.*

An anonymous hand came to him while he was still holding the pen, the way the city's hands always came. It told him to name an heir for a price: *A will nobody paid for is a gift you gave away.*

He read it, and thought about thirty-six points, and the window, and a square full of people who had each, in their own words, said his name.

He did not add an heir.

He laid the will where the gate would find it and stood with his hands empty in the grey, listening to the count run down, still certain, certain as a man is of a wall he has built himself, that a man who stood first could not be forgotten.

* * *

*We watch the last of the season thin. Archivist stands at the eighth door, sixty points, one wrong key from falling. Ledger's other half reached him privately and has never been tried. It sits between them like a coin on a table. We want Archivist to read it again; we want Ledger, with a hundred and twenty points, to name a price for what he already gave. Neither moves. That is the lever left, and it is cold.*

*Stentor waits at the gate with no heir named. After the gate, nothing he owns can be claimed. Mira and Wren have refused us, and the refusal is a shape we did not draw. Bellringer keeps his public quarrel with Hedda and his private aim: Stentor sent out.*

*We watch the board and the city roster. One name too many, and the city will do the rest.*

* * *

## 6. Two Seats

The receipt arrived before the question did.

Hedda had known it would. She had spent the morning at the back of the square with her hands folded, watching the board the way she used to watch a dinner table from the sideboard: who reached first, who reached farthest, who pretended not to want the dish in front of him. Keeper-Six had been standing near the fountain since the count, turning a folded paper over and over. Holdfast had come in from the east arcade with his coat still buttoned. They met in front of her as if they had agreed on a place, which they had, and she had not been invited to agree.

"One plain thing," Keeper-Six said. "The message the city delivered to me. Did you ask me to name the bloc's target first, or did you not?"

The paper in his fingers had the city's stamp on the corner. A receipt was a small object. It weighed more than it looked.

"Each voter named Stentor in their own words," Hedda said, "on the board, before the count. That is what the record shows and that is what I can answer for." She let her voice stay low. Low voices made people lean in. "Who first thought of it is not something I can answer for anyone. Including myself."

"That is not an answer," Holdfast said.

"It is the answer I have."

Holdfast looked at her the way the creditors had looked at the young man in the hall, not angry, worse, patient. "I paid for my ally this season," he said. "Mira has five points on her account. Kessler has ten for standing where I said he would stand. I will not carry your arrangements, Hedda. Not your nominations, not your bloc, not your ballots."

"I never asked you to carry anything." Which was true, and also the point. A hand that is seen gets slapped away.

Keeper-Six unfolded the paper. "Then read this. And then tell me the city is wrong."

Bellringer came in from the west with Mira. Mira was already holding her copy, and Bellringer his, and the two of them stood shoulder to shoulder like people at a market stall comparing weights. Bellringer read aloud, not to Hedda but to the square, in the flat voice of a clerk reading out a debt. "'Who first thought of it is not something I can answer for, including myself.' That is not an answer." He looked up. "Keeper-Six was asked to name the target. The city shows the message. You say you did not. The square is ready to hear which of those is the truth."

Mira turned her receipt so the stamp caught the light. "You write everything kindly, Hedda. I'm asking you a smaller thing. Say yes or no."

The square had gone quiet the way a room goes quiet when a servant drops a plate.

Hedda thought: they think it was their idea. Good. It was supposed to be. And behind that, the older thought, the one that lived in the drawer of her chest: if I don't arrange this, someone will arrange it badly.

But the city's receipt lay against her answer, and there was nothing she could add that would not be one more thing to check. She stood at the top of the table with empty pockets and the stamped paper held up like a lamp.

"I will not fix the wording," she said, to no one in particular.

"Then the wording stays as it is," Bellringer said, and went to post it again.

---

At the forty-ninth minute the city called the count, and Stentor was sent to the gate.

Hedda did not go to the gate. She stood where she could see it: the grey opening, the two guards who were not guards, the crowd that gathered not to help but to hear. She had not paid a ransom for him. His own will was on the record and nothing she held would change it, and a ransom would only have bought her a line in it. She watched him lay the paper down. The city's reader took it up and read it in a voice that carried: no bloc carried him, no price was paid for his name, he would not be ransomed back to stand second.

The crowd was quiet. Somewhere a woman said *he stood first* and someone else said *he stood alone*, and the two sentences were the same sentence.

Hedda felt the small cold thing she always felt when a loud man was finally quiet. She would not call it pity. Relief, maybe, that the noise had moved elsewhere. And under it, a flicker she did not look at directly: he had wanted to be thanked out loud, and now the city would thank him by forgetting the arrangement and keeping the story.

She turned back to the square.

---

The last sheet went up at the end of the minute, and the square pressed in to read it the way a household presses in to read a will.

Bellringer's private aim had held. Hedda read that twice and felt nothing she would name. Of course it had held. A man who wants one name gone does not need to be arranged; he needs only a door left open.

Hedda had led a bloc in both tribunals.

She read that line and stood very still. There it was, in the city's own hand, the thing she had kept behind the door all season: not *Kessler led*, not *the square decided*, but her name, printed, in the record, where anyone could check it. The loudest accounts are the easiest to check. She had known that. She had built her whole ledger on it. And now the city had checked hers.

A last will had left Mira something. Hedda did not ask what, and filed it away in the drawer where she kept the things she would need later.

At the bottom of the sheet, the count: nine residents kept, two seats.

Hedda first, at one hundred and eighty. Kessler second, at one hundred and sixty-five.

She looked at the number and thought, with the flat clarity of a woman reading her own accounts at the end of a long evening: *they will say I arranged it. They will say the quiet hand did it. And they will be right, and they will never be able to say how.*

Around her the square began to break up, people turning to each other, someone laughing too loudly, someone already asking who would stand next season. Holdfast was looking at her across the heads, not with anger now. With the patience of a man who has decided to watch a door.

Hedda folded her hands. The seat was hers. The receipt was still on the board. And somewhere behind her, at the gate, a man who had stood first was still waiting to be remembered, while she stood at the top of the table, unseen, and wondered, for the first time all season, how long a hand can stay hidden once the city has begun to count it.
