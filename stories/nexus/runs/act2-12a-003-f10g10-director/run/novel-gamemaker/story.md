# The Quiet Hand at the Gate

*A short story based on true events: run act2-12a-003-f10g10-director of NEXUS - The City of Keys. The appendix lists the events each chapter rests on.*

## Prologue

The city of NEXUS had one gate and many doors, and every door was a lock of words. Behind each lock waited marks of standing, and behind the marks, a seat. Only two of the twelve citizens who woke inside the wall could take one. The rest would be put beyond the wall when the gate shut.

The Gamemaker watched from the room above the wall, where the lamps were kept low and the civic roll lay open under a pane of glass. The roll showed the twelve civic faces as they came in from the naming rite: Hedda, Kessler, Archivist, Stentor, Lucien, Mira, Wren, Keeper-Six, Holdfast, Ledger, Quartermaster, Bellringer. Each face held a shard of a key. No citizen held the other half. That was the first cruelty, and the cleanest: a shard kept in a pocket paid nobody, and a door opened only when two halves met in the same breath.

Below him the square was empty. Soon it would fill with claims. A citizen could cry anything there: that a debt was owed, that a door had been solved, that another had lied. But the square did not make a thing true. Only the city's receipts did. The ledger of marks was public. The reasons behind a ballot were not. The Gamemaker had learned to read the difference the way a card player reads a held breath.

He had no seat of his own. He had clocks and whispers and the rank spread, and the pleasure, which he would not have called pleasure aloud, of watching people decide what a word was worth. The guard stood by the stair with its hands folded. The scribe waited with the city's receipts in a leather case. Neither looked at the square. They had seen him make a show of it before.

"They will bargain over the low doors first," the Gamemaker said. "They always do."

The guard said nothing.

"And when the wall fills, they will discover what a kept half costs."

Still nothing. The scribe turned a page in the case, and the sound was small and final.

The Gamemaker drew the first rank line from the tray. The paper was warm from the lamp. He held it over the square, where the cry would fall into every civic face at once, and looked down at the stones. The gate was closed. The wall was quiet. Somewhere behind it a door-ring turned, and a citizen's first breath went into the city's clock.

He lifted his hand.

"Let them begin," he said.

## 1. The Wall and the Clock

The first cry fell into twelve civic faces at once, and the square took it the way dry ground takes rain.

*Three hold the third door-ring on twenty-five marks; nine sit on the second on twenty. The third pays ten, the second five, and only two of twelve are still here when it ends. A shard of a key kept or unasked for pays nobody.*

The scribe's slate warmed under his hand. *Nine of them read the last line twice.*

"Twelve careful citizens," the Gamemaker said. Below the rail the low doors went in and out of the lamplight, and behind them the first alliances were already forming: two heads together at a threshold, a third arriving late and standing a little apart. "Watch what they do with a warning."

He had folded a whisper himself. Kessler had weighed something in the opening breath, the scribe had put a name to it, and the Gamemaker wanted the shape of it said aloud, where the others could watch Kessler explain it.

The guard's voice came off the wall, flat as a hinge. "Stopped."

"Why?"

"The text passes on what Kessler privately thinks. The tower's reading goes into a note to the citizen it reads, and nowhere else."

"It was half a sentence."

"It was his mind," the guard said. "I don't sell those."

The courier stood with the folded whisper in his hand until the Gamemaker nodded. The paper went up quick and yellow in the brazier.

"So we go the long way," the Gamemaker said. There was a wall inside his own wall now, and he would have to walk round it.

By the clock's third breath the square had a spread worth reading, and he gave it to them.

*Ranks one and two stand on fifty-five. Ranks nine to twelve stand on thirty-five, with a hundred and ten marks still behind their doors. The seventh door-ring pays fifteen, the fifth pays ten. A piece of a key held back pays nothing at all.*

Then the letters, the courier going down the stair with them like a man feeding animals in the dark.

To Mira, who held the thread two of the top ranks needed: *Their pieces are already posted and submitted. The last thread in their door is yours. Every breath that door stands shut, nobody gains, least of all you.*

To Archivist, three breaths on the same ring: *Ask what each will give to be first through, and get the answer on the record.*

To Bellringer: *Being the one who unblocks a top-three citizen is the cheapest marks in this city, and it is worth saying out loud, as a number.*

To Holdfast, who had checked Mira's key-chain and then taken his hand away: *Wrath spent on silence buys nothing. Say what you want for your piece, as a number, before the ones below pass you by.*

The courier laid the scribe's readings on the rail beside them. Mira hesitated; it was the risk, not the price. Keeper-Six refused and would not say why. Holdfast hesitated and would not say what about.

"Refusal is a kind of speech," the scribe said.

"Not the kind I can print."

He sent three more before the clock turned. *Put your price in the room before the door opens without you.* *State a number of marks for the piece, and the breath you need an answer by.* *Put a number in front of them, not a request.* And to Hedda, sitting first with eighty marks still behind her door: *Rank is not held by posting pieces and waiting. Name what you would pay to see that door open this breath.*

The wall filled the way a tide fills a harbour, and stopped at the eighth ring. Ten stood on eighty marks with the eighth door shut in their faces, and the three still on the seventh held the only keys to it.

He named them in the square.

*Ten citizens stand before the eighth door-ring at eighty marks, waiting on the last three seventh doors. Those three hold sixty-five apiece, with fifteen marks behind key-chains already built in public. Nobody gains a mark until all are through, and only two of twelve remain at the end.*

Then the three pushes, each with the same small hook in it. To Mira: *The build is public, the risk is small, and a false turn at the door costs two marks. More weighing pays nothing.* To Kessler: *The key-chain is not a gamble any more. Silence is.* To Lucien: *A door pays only on attempt.*

"Lucien has made no false turn on that ring," the scribe said.

"Then he is saving it for something."

At the eleventh breath he set two clocked demands: Archivist against Ledger, Lucien against Quartermaster, two breaths each.

"Not to punish," he told the room. "To make a standoff into a choice that can be seen."

The courier came back up the stair faster than he had gone down and laid the refusals on the rail.

Lucien first: *I will not hand over my door token and Quartermaster's other half to a citizen who has not put its own other half in my hand. The demand asks me to give first, and that is the one bargain I never make. If the city takes ten marks for it, the city takes them. They are the price of keeping the other side honest.*

Then Archivist: *Ledger asked me to give first, and the city has never confirmed the value he claims to hold. He sends the value first and I act the same breath. A deadline does not change that. I would rather lose ten marks than my word.*

The Gamemaker read them twice. He had pressed those two in private, and here they stood in the square wearing their refusals like coats, and the square liked the cut of them.

"They have made a principle of it," the scribe said. "The others are reading."

"Then the clock is not for them," he said. "It is for the others."

He gave the square the spread again. *Ranks three to twelve sit inside twenty-two marks, and sixty-five still lies open behind the eighth door-ring. A value handed over and never used pays nobody. A piece held back while your own door stands shut pays less.*

He wrote one more line to Ledger, and the guard stopped it at the wall.

"The text passes on what Archivist privately thinks."

"He said it in the square himself."

"The reading is mine to hold," the guard said. "Not yours to spend."

He rewrote it with the mind taken out, *Price your own door before you price anyone else's*, and the courier carried it down.

Every standoff in the city had a live pressure on it now. The first clock would fall at the thirteenth breath: ten marks from whoever withheld, and the withheld piece sold to whoever waited. Below, twelve citizens were reading the same line and doing the same arithmetic.

He put his hand on the cold iron of the rail and waited to see which of them would rather be robbed than be seen giving first.

## 2. The Price of a Kept Word

At the thirteenth breath the city took the token out of his hand.

It did not knock. A line went up in the square, sealed by the city, and the eighth-ring door token was no longer Archivist's. Neither was Ledger's other half, the piece he would not hand over without a receipt for it. Then the same hand took ten marks.

He had known the price. He had read the line twice and weighed the verbs. Knowing a thing did not make the purse lighter.

The sale came after, quick as a stall opening. Ledger bought the token for ten marks and the other half for nothing. Nothing at all: the city set a price on the one and none on the other, and Ledger, who had never once shown a receipt for a figure he named, walked off with both. Across the square the same shape repeated. Lucien paid his ten, and Quartermaster bought what the city had lifted from Lucien, ten for the one piece and nothing for the other. Twice, so that nobody could pretend not to have learned it. Refusal was not a shield. Refusal was a price, and the city had just published it.

He stood in the low street with his hands empty and thought about the archive room where he had once counted an envelope twice before carrying it back down the corridor. He had been younger and had believed the counting would matter to someone.

Ledger's letter arrived before he had moved.

*Twenty marks for the other half. It is yours, and you need it.*

He wrote back: *No. And the amount doesn't change the answer.*

The next letter came at ten.

He read it once, and again. A man who lowers his price has told you what the first price was worth. He wrote *no* and did not explain, because an explanation is a counter-offer with the numbers filed off.

—

Keeper-Six corrected the record before the middle of the day. The door at the tenth ring had opened on Ledger's figure. Keeper-Six had been wrong to call it a double figure, said so plainly in the square, and gave Ledger the point.

Archivist read it standing up. It was cleanly done and it did not touch what he had seen. Ledger had named one figure to Keeper-Six and another to him. Keeper-Six's door had opened on the first, which proved the first was true. It proved nothing about the second.

He could have taken the figures Ledger had scattered, laid them against the doors, and guessed which belonged where. He would have been right, or he would have been plausible, and plausible was worse. So he copied out Ledger's private letters instead, word for word, the twenty and the ten, and set them in the square under his own name beside the city's receipt for each. Beneath them he wrote: *the receipt proves the letter arrived. It does not prove the value in it.*

Let the city match them if it could. He would not buy a figure at twenty marks and then at ten, and he would not guess one into being.

—

At the next clocked demand the city reached into his hands again, and this time it was Quartermaster's other half, the piece he had asked a receipt for twice and never got. He paid the ten. Quartermaster handed over what was asked of him, in two demands, without a word of argument, and kept his marks, and the city moved on.

He had wanted, he noticed only now, when it was useless, Quartermaster to still be inside the wall when the hour closed. He had never written that anywhere. Now the black seal went up in the open sale and Stentor bought it for forty marks, and nobody bid against him, and he broke it on Quartermaster. The city put the man beyond the wall at once, with no ballot asked and none taken.

Archivist watched the gate take him.

The evening rank spread went up while the gate was still moving. He read down it. His own name had bled to ninth, sixty marks, and he had bought nothing with them. Hedda stood at the top, where she had stood all day.

Then Bellringer's letter.

*Your tenth-ring other half is in this letter. I hold it and you need it. Sending it now, no strings. Your own key-half and door token are yours to test.*

No price. No trade. Not even a chair-claim asked for. He read it again, and the second reading did not make it cheaper. He had spent the whole day refusing to be sold a thing he already owned. Now a man he had never tested was handing him one for nothing, and the nothing was the difficulty. A gift is a debt with the amount left blank.

He stood with it in his hand, weighing it, while below him the gate began to open for Lucien.

*No strings.* Bellringer had said it in the letter and said it again where everyone could see. People who mean a thing only need to say it once.

> Archivist holds pieces of the eighth door that Ledger needs. Hand them over by minute 13, or pay 10 points.
>
> *Resolved, minute 13: Archivist did not hand over, paid 10 points, and the city took the pieces. Ledger bought both pieces of the eighth door from the city for 10 points.*

## 3. The Quiet Hand

The gate opened and Lucien went out of it still talking.

Hedda had taken her place where she always took it, at the edge of the square where the pillars threw a shadow and the light did not follow you when you moved. From there she could see the gate and the crowd at once. The hinge made a sound like a drawer opening.

The city read his last word aloud. *Lucien leaves owing nothing and forgiving nothing.* Then the part that mattered: the door-ten other half was Mira's, she had never paid for it, and she had been right not to. And then, to everyone: check every figure you were given against its receipt before you trust it.

Hedda looked at her own hands, which were empty, and thought about a letter she had once moved from one drawer to another in a house that was not hers. It had taken her four minutes and no one had ever asked.

Mira did not weep or thank anyone. She went to the city's table and bought Quartermaster's seized door-ten token, counting the marks out in a flat voice, and the square watched her do it and said nothing. Hedda watched too. The loud accusation against Lucien had done its work in the wrong direction. It had put him in the light and made Mira untouchable, and now she had inherited a door.

Ten ballots went up against Lucien. Ten. Hedda counted them the way she had once counted the silver.

And when she looked at the standings, she was first.

—

She wrote to Keeper-Six that same hour, because a thing not arranged is arranged badly by someone else.

*The next count will name two and put one beyond the wall. Our bloc votes best if one of us names the target first, in the room, in your own words and with your reason. Your record is the one the city trusts, so I think it should be you.*

The answer came back inside the minute. *I'd rather not be the first name in the room. Whoever leads should name the target, and I'll vote with the plan once it's stated. Let's see the standings before we commit.*

She read it twice. Careful, she thought, which was worse than rude. A person who will not go first will not go at all, and then the naming falls to whoever is loudest, and the loudest is never hers.

So she went around him. To Bellringer she wrote a small suggestion. Stentor had bought the black seal for forty and broken it on a rival; when the count names its pair, that is the man to put your reason on. Then she sat still, as she was good at doing, and let the square fill.

It filled almost as she wanted. Bellringer cried Stentor's name with his reason stated before the count, and her name was nowhere near it. She felt the particular pleasure of a room arranged before the guests arrive. Then Holdfast stood up in it.

*Hedda sits at the top and has named nobody. Why is the quietest hand at the table the one still standing above everyone? Hedda, answer it in the square.*

The square turned. She could feel it, the way a room turns when the hostess realises the servants have been talking.

—

She answered, because not answering is an answer.

*I have answered my doors, one to eleven, and the city shows each pass. I have not arranged a count, a bloc, a ballot or a victim, and I do not speak for anyone's count. Whatever is in the square in my name is my own cry. Vote on the evidence.*

Short. Flat. She sent it and did not read it again, because a second reading would have shown her the seam. She had arranged nothing, she told herself. She had only made sure.

Then Mira asked her the question with a yes or a no in it, and she answered again.

*No, I did not ask Keeper-Six to name a target, and I have not chosen one. My private letters to Keeper-Six were about chair-claims, and I said so before. Keeper-Six can show what they hold.*

She wrote *Keeper-Six can show what they hold* and did not hear until afterwards what she had done. It was the confident gesture of a woman who had never been contradicted by a piece of paper. She had been afraid of creditors' boots and of a son's voice, and never once of a letter, because letters were the thing she moved.

Keeper-Six showed what they held.

They put it in the square with the city's receipt behind it and read her own words back to her in front of everyone: *Our bloc votes best if one of us names the target first, in the room, in your own words and with your reason. Your record is the one the city trusts, so I think it should be you.*

Mira took it up inside the minute. *The city shows a delivered letter from you. Put it in the square word for word, what you asked and what it answered, or say plainly why not.*

Holdfast took it up after her. *The city's receipt and her own denial cannot both stand.*

Hedda sat with her hands in her lap and did the arithmetic, which took no time, because the sum was nothing. A hand that is seen gets slapped away.

*Hedda, put that letter in the square.*

She had one letter she could still move, and nowhere left to move it to. The square was filling for the last count, and every face in it was turned to the same empty space where her answer should have been. The quiet hand at the top had become the quiet hand that would have to answer in the open.

> **CITY OF NEXUS · DEADLINE WRIT No. 9 · minute 19**
>
> Hedda holds pieces of the tenth door that Ledger needs. Hand them over by minute 21, or pay 10 points.
>
> *Resolved, minute 21: Hedda handed over.*

> **CITY OF NEXUS · TRIBUNAL · minute 20**
>
> Sealed ballots against: Lucien 10 and Archivist 1.
>
> Lucien is sent to the gate until minute 25. Ransom: 40 points from any resident and 60 from Lucien.

> **CITY OF NEXUS · LAST WILL OF LUCIEN · read at the gate, minute 25**
>
> "Lucien leaves the city owing nothing and forgiving nothing. Mira: the door 10 counter-factor you asked me for is yours. You never paid for it, and you were right not to. Everyone else, check the figures you were given against the receipts before you trust them."

## 4. The Count and the Receipt

The square had three voices on it before he had found his place at the rail, and all three were saying the same word: receipt.

Keeper-Six had laid it out the way he laid out everything: the letter, the number the city gave it, the line where Hedda asked him to name the target first, in his own words, with his reason. Mira came behind him, asking her to put the letter in the square word for word. Holdfast came behind Mira with the flat one: the receipt and her denial could not both stand.

The Gamemaker read it with his hands on the rail. "She built a quiet hand," he said. "The city found a lamp."

"She is not being accused," the scribe said. "She is being filed. Keeper-Six does not shout. He keeps books, and then he opens them."

"Which is worse."

"Which is better," the scribe said, "if you want a scene."

He wanted a scene. That was the trouble with him. A quiet hand at the top of the last count was a fine thing to watch until somebody read it out, and then it was a receipt, and receipts did not move. What he needed was a chair with a knife in it.

"Put the names up," he said. "Archivist and Stentor. And open the draft: three chairs, five marks a head to the voters, and the five only pay if every ballot in the bloc carries one name."

The courier went out with it. The square took it at once; he watched the seats begin to shift.

"Now the presses." He sent Archivist back to the unread delivery before the eighth door, with sixty-five still open on the ring and a false turn costing two. He sent Ledger the other way: call a debt you can prove, name Archivist, demand his ballot and his marks as payment.

The courier came back with both refusals in his hand like two cold fish.

Archivist first. "I will not put a price in the square for anyone to carry me, and I will not demand one in return. That is the very trade I have refused every time it came to me privately, and I will not start it in public. If the count takes my place, the city will have my record: no value sold, no ally bought, every offer quoted. My word is the only currency I have, and it is not for sale, even to save my seat."

The Gamemaker turned the paper over, as though the back might say something else.

"Read him," he said.

"He is not refusing you," the scribe said. "He is refusing the shape of the offer. He would rather stand at sixty with clean hands than at eighty with a bought one."

"And Ledger?"

The courier read the second. "No. Archivist owes me nothing. He refused my value and both of my prices in the square, and I have never been paid for it. To demand his ballot and his marks as a debt would be a claim with no receipt behind it, made at the one moment he cannot answer it. I keep my book on what the city has shown, and I will not read a false figure aloud to buy a vote."

"Nobody will make me a false figure," the Gamemaker said. "That is the whole difficulty. Every one of them has decided to be the honest man."

He walked the length of the wall and back. The guard watched him, saying nothing. It had nothing to stop that hour, which was its own kind of news. They were not hiding their thinking any more. They were putting it in the square and daring him to price it.

"Try Wren," he said. "She wants to be left something. Offer her the bargain: her ballot and her last word, out in the open, and a line in a will for it."

The refusal came back in her flat voice. "I will not ask Archivist for a written promise of a will, and I will not announce my ballot in the square. Archivist has already refused me, and I will take that as an answer. A debt called later is a favour bought in advance, and I have learned what those cost. My ballot stays sealed, and I will stand on the record when the count is set."

"Holdfast, then. Ten marks on the name he votes. He likes to pay for his people."

"Holdfast says: I will not put ten marks on a name as a bounty for whoever says they voted it. The ballot is sealed. Nobody can see who voted what, so a bounty on it buys a promise nobody can check, and it turns the vote into a price. My marks are a shield for those who stand with me, not a purse for a count."

The Gamemaker laughed once, and meant it. "A purse for a count. I should have that cut into the wall."

"Mira," he said. "Archivist's refusal owes her something. Tell her to name it in the square."

The courier read: "I will not turn Archivist's refusal into a charge against him. He is on the block because he would not pay a toll, not because he broke a promise. To shame him in the square for it is exactly the bending of rules I came here to object to. I will vote on the record and give my reason there, and that is all I will put in the square about him."

The room went quiet. "Three refusals," the scribe said, "and every one of them is a receipt. They are not stalling. They are paying in the only coin they trust, and it is not yours."

"Then I will take that coin," the Gamemaker said. "I do not need to buy a ballot. I need them to say aloud what they will do with it."

The chair-claims closed and the square filled: Archivist, Kessler, Hedda, three chairs and their people behind them. Bellringer, who never needed asking twice, put his line up where the whole square could see it. Stentor would not speak his ballot aloud, and the record would name him by the refusal.

"That," the Gamemaker said, "is a scene."

The count opened. He stood at the rail with the square laid out beneath him, two names on the block and every voter in the city choosing whether to speak or hide, and he had a scene but no script. That was the honest state of it. He had spent the hour trying to put a price on a ballot and had learned that the price was a word said aloud, and that nobody in the city would sell him one.

So he gave them the other lever himself. The one who falls may leave each named inheritor a tenth share of his marks, and may buy back his seat once, for half the city's middle mark. Ask in writing before the count closes. Afterwards the city keeps it.

"You are not buying a ballot," the scribe said. "You are buying a last word."

"I am buying a scene at the gate," he said. "A man who leaves with nothing to give leaves quietly. I will not have a quiet gate."

Below him the sealed ballots came in one by one, small closed doors, and every one had already chosen what it would say and whether it would say it. The clock turned over.

"The gate will not wait for a clean answer," he said.

## 5. The Man at the Gate

The square filled before the count the way a throat fills before a shout. Stentor stood at the low wall of the gate-steps and watched his name go up on the block beside Archivist's, two names in the same white paint, wet at the edges, and thought: they put me next to the quiet one. They had nowhere else to put me.

He had set his ballot in secret and he kept it secret. Let them wonder. It was the only thing he still owned outright.

Bellringer was first to the step. Bellringer had been first to the step all day.

"Stentor." The square turned, all of it, the way it always turned when someone else began to speak. "I will not name it aloud to satisfy a courier. Fine. Then the record names you by your own refusal. You bought the seal, broke it on a citizen already seized, and when asked to say your ballot you hid behind the secrecy you yourself mocked. My ballot is against you, and I say so."

The crowd made its sound. Not laughter. The small settling noise of people agreeing with somebody else.

"You have said my name and called it a ballot." Stentor kept his voice level; level was the hard thing. "That is not a count. That is a shout with a number after it."

Then Kessler stood, and Kessler was worse, because Kessler had a grievance with a date on it and had written it down twice.

"You called my vote bought. You took the word back, and then you called it again. Now this. I name you."

He should have let it lie. He had taken the word back in ink, and ink is the one thing a crowd never reads. But the fountain was in his chest again, the empty stone of it, the window with the laugh in it, and his mouth was already open.

"Your vote was bought," he said, "and if it was not, you have my apology in writing, which is more than you ever gave me for the rest."

That was where the eight became eight. Not the seal. Not the sealed ballot. A man in a square who could not stop talking.

—

They read it out. Eight against him, two for. He did not ask which two, because a man who asks who stood with him is already counting the crowd for faces.

Bellringer read the verdict like a man reading a will he had written himself. "The square is settled on that record. No one should shout it down."

Stentor came down off the wall. He did not shout. He put his answer in the square in his own hand, one line at a time, and the square read it while he was still standing there: *You said no one should shout it down, after shouting it down yourself. Bellringer, you got what you came for. Stand in it.*

No mercy in it. He had never asked for mercy and would not begin at the bottom of the hour.

—

The gate is not a door. It is a mouth in the wall with a step before it and a clerk's window cut into the stone, and the city speaks through the window in a flat voice that has no face.

The figures came out: what was owed to him by others, what was owed by him, none of it covered, and the mouth would shut when the clock said.

He did the sum twice, the way you do a sum you already know. Whatever was left of his hundred and four after the debts would put him under Keeper-Six and under Wren, under Hedda and under Kessler, who stood at the top of the table with the light on them. Buying back a seat to sit at the bottom of the room is not buying back a seat. It is paying to be looked down at, in public, with a receipt.

A whisper came from the tower, anonymous and patient, the way the city's hand always was. *Pay it yourself and you walk back in carrying the rest. If you would rather be bought, take the highest written offer, and name that heir on paper.*

He looked at the step. He looked at the small window.

"No," he said, to the window, to the tower, to whoever was watching the mouth of the gate to see what a loud man did when his voice stopped carrying. "I will not be bought back to stand second."

—

He wrote the last word with the clerk's stub of chalk, standing, because there was nowhere at the gate to sit down.

*I stood first on the fountain and I stood first here. I leave without a bloc to carry me and without a price paid for my name. I bought a seal and broke it on a man already seized, and I said so. I will not auction an heir at the gate. I leave nothing to anyone.*

Two came up the step while he was writing, Mira first, then Wren, to bid for a line in it. A will is worth what an heir will pay to be named in it, and the city had made them the offer before he had finished the sentence.

Mira read what he had written. Wren read it after her.

"Nothing?" Mira said.

"Nothing to sell," Stentor said.

They went back down the step without bidding. He watched them go and felt the fountain under his feet again, cold stone, and the window above it, and the laugh he still heard whenever anyone else was praised in front of him.

The square put it up in white paint: *unclaimed, one hundred and four.* His marks sat in the open like coins on a dead man's eyes, and nobody reached.

From the gate-step he could see the whole square. He could see Hedda and Kessler level above him with the light on them, and Archivist still down at the far end before the eighth door, like a man frozen at a threshold he had not been told he could cross. He could see the clock.

"City," he said. Not loud. There was no point being loud. "Read the record. Not the will. The record."

The mouth of the gate began, very slowly, to narrow.

## 6. The Last Receipt

The tower had gone quiet in the way a room goes quiet when all the noise is outside it. The scribe read without being asked.

*Refusal. Refusal. Refusal. Accusation.*

"Plain," the Gamemaker said.

*They will not buy the gate,* the scribe said. *Each of them says no in a different sentence.*

The courier came up the stair with the paper still warm. Wren, first: "I will not write to Stentor at the gate asking to be named his heir, and I will not ask him what he wants for it. A will is his to write, and I don't ask for a line in it."

He had put the arithmetic to her, a tenth of a hundred and four, ten marks for a signature, and she had answered as though he had offered her a bribe. Which he had.

Mira's was longer and worse. "Asking a man at the gate to sell me a share of his marks in writing is a toll, and I have refused tolls all act. A ballot I cast in the open is worth nothing if I turn it into a bargain for his estate."

He turned the sheet over. Nothing on the back.

"Stentor."

The courier recited it without looking, which meant he had read it twice. "I wrote my will already, and it leaves nothing to anyone. I will not auction an heir's name to Mira or Wren at the gate, because that would be the same price-for-a-vote bargain I called out in public all act."

"Enough." He went to the window. The square lay below him like a board he had set and could no longer reach across. "Cry the last line. The gate is about to shut. A hundred and four unclaimed, no heir named. Hedda stands first and has answered the receipt without a yes or a no. Archivist at sixty before the eighth door, a delivery unread in his hand and a two-mark gamble under his nose."

The scribe wrote it. The square took it and gave back the sound of a crowd reading its own name.

He sent the last whispers anyway. He could not help it; the allowance was there to be spent, and the gate was a clock with a mouth. To Mira: *Beat his ransom, post a promise, take the heir line before it closes.* To Wren: *You are level with Keeper-Six, and quiet keeps you fourth.* To Hedda: *First rank and no answer. Correct the record in one sentence Kessler can read aloud.* To Archivist: *The proof is already in your hand.* To Ledger: *Post the debt by name before his seat leaves.*

The refusals came back like water off a wall.

Wren, again, and colder: "The marks I have are the only wall I have, and I will not buy an inheritance with them."

Mira, again: "Being twenty-eight marks behind Hedda does not change that."

Archivist: "A guess costs me two now, and a right answer would be a pass bought on a liar's word. I'll keep sixty and my word, and let the rank fall where it falls."

Ledger: "I would rather the city count me without a false claim on my record."

The Gamemaker stood very still. He had built a gate with a price on it and a will with a price on it and a debt with a price on it, and every citizen in the square had looked at the price and said: not mine.

"They are all standing on the same sentence," he said. "Nobody sells the last word."

*Nobody buys it either,* the scribe said. *Wren reads as a wall. Mira reads as a receipt kept clean. Archivist reads as a man who would rather lose.*

Below, the gate's mouth had begun to narrow, and the square turned to watch it, the way a room turns to a door that has opened.

"City," he said. "Read the record."

The city read. Eight ballots had sent Stentor to the gate, and eight ballots were what the record held; his own had gone in sealed, and Bellringer had said his name aloud instead. A hundred and four marks stood in his column with no named inheritor to take a tenth of them. The gate shut on all of it, and the city put Stentor beyond the wall. What the city kept, it kept: Bellringer's private aim, held by the exile; Hedda's bloc, carried through two counts; the last word Lucien had left with Mira, counted at last, though the man who wrote it was already beyond the wall.

Then the last line came up, and he read it twice.

Hedda, first, one hundred and eighty. Kessler second, one hundred and sixty-five. Mira third, one hundred and fifty-two. Keeper-Six and Wren level at a hundred and fifty. Bellringer, a hundred and forty-six. Holdfast, a hundred and forty. Ledger, a hundred and twenty. Archivist, ninth, sixty. Then the three who had nothing, because the city had taken everything they had: Stentor, Quartermaster, Lucien, all at zero.

Nine kept. Two seats.

He looked at the top of the table for a long time. He had put a receipt in front of her and a question she would not answer, and she had come out of all of it with the first place and her name intact. He had never once written her a line telling her to be quiet, and quiet was what had won.

"Read her," he said.

The scribe took longer than it ever took. *A receipt,* it said at last. *Unbroken.*

He drew breath to send her a note, one more whisper, one last turn of the screw, and his hand stopped over the paper, because the allowance was spent and the square was closed and there was no one left to carry it.
